0 Executive summary
The project asks a simple but surprisingly untested question: if a user’s emotional context changes,
should the navigation structure change with it? Most e-commerce sites keep one menu architecture
for everyone. Most adaptive-menu research changes item order, highlights, or recommendations inside
a fixed structure. This project compares six genuinely different structures — horizontal bar, vertical
sidebar, mega menu, fractal zoom, rounded-square icon grid, and radial lattice — under four
experimentally induced emotional contexts, then tests whether switching structure on the basis of that
mapping actually improves product finding.

0.1 The scientific gap
Four literatures meet here but do not yet connect. Menu research shows that breadth, depth, layout, and visual
format change search speed and errors (Miller, 1981; Kiger, 1984; Leuthold et al., 2011; Chiu & Yang, 2024).
Adaptive-interface research shows that personalisation can improve satisfaction and engagement, but also that
reordering and switching create predictability and learning costs (Findlater & McGrenere, 2004; Gajos et al., 2008;
Lavie & Meyer, 2010; Gaspar-Figueiredo et al., 2025). Emotion research shows that affect shapes information
processing and search persistence (Russell, 1980; Schwarz & Clore, 1983; Isen, 2001). Navigation research shows that
disorientation and frustration are central failure modes (Lazar et al., 2003; Smith, 1996). What is missing is a
controlled test that treats whole menu structure as the adaptive variable and emotional context as the moderator.
The existing protocol (v3.0) proposed four menus and four emotions. This plan extends it in five ways: (1) adds the
rounded-square icon grid and the radial lattice, giving six structures that cover linear, panel, recursive-spatial, icon-
grid, and radial geometries; (2) formalises the 6 × 4 design, randomisation, and target rotation; (3) replaces
optimistic sample sizes with a simulation-based power analysis; (4) adds a causal test that separates matching from
mere switching; and (5) adds an explicit estimator-error simulation so the end-to-end study can answer “how
accurate must emotion inference be before adaptation pays off?”
0.2 The four emotional contexts
We keep happiness / positive affect and frustration, and add two states that are specifically diagnostic for
navigation: confusion / lostness and boredom / disengagement. Together they span the two dimensions of the
affect circumplex (valence and arousal) plus two states that matter uniquely for wayfinding: uncertainty about where
something is (confusion) and low motivation to keep looking (boredom). Anxiety and curiosity were considered but
rejected for the core design; §3.2 explains why.
0.3 The six menus
All six render the same four departments, three subcategories per department, six leaf styles per subcategory, and
the same 72 fictional watches. The running example is Sport & Dive → Pro Diver 300m → Titanium Deep.

What makes this a paper rather than a prototype demo
The paper does not assume “frustrated users need a sidebar.” Study 1 estimates the 6 × 4 mapping. Study 2 then freezes that
mapping and tests whether using it beats (a) the best static menu and (b) a deliberately mismatched menu. Study 3 tests the
complete system with a noisy estimator and a safe transition policy. A null result — for example, one menu wins everywhere, or
switching costs cancel the gains — is a publishable finding because it answers the applied question “is emotion-contingent
structural adaptation worth it?”

1 Background and related work
This project sits at the intersection of menu design, adaptive user interfaces, and affective computing.
The literature is rich in each area, but the three are rarely combined in a single controlled experiment.
This section summarises what is established, what the four supplied papers contribute, and where the
present study adds new evidence.
1.1 Menu structure is not a neutral container
Decades of menu research show that the shape of a hierarchy changes search behaviour. The classic breadth–depth
trade-off finds that broader, shallower menus are faster and less error-prone than deep, narrow ones, at least within
the ranges tested (Miller, 1981; Kiger, 1984; Snowberry et al., 1983). Two-level menus often outperform deeper
hierarchies (Parkinson et al., 1988), which is one reason the mega menu — a panel that exposes several levels at
once — can be efficient (Li & Nielsen, 2017). But breadth is not free: a panel with many links increases visual density
and the number of distractors, and it can make category boundaries harder to parse.
Orientation matters too. Leuthold et al. (2011) used eye tracking to compare vertical, dynamic, and simple menus
and found that vertical menus required less visual effort and produced faster, more successful navigation. Chiu and
Yang (2024) extended this to e-commerce menu formats, comparing a horizontal main menu with a vertical local
menu and a fully horizontal version. Their eye-tracking data showed that a horizontal main menu plus a vertical local
menu improved visual efficiency and satisfaction relative to a dense, multi-category horizontal layout. The practical
lesson is not “vertical is always better”; it is that the fit between orientation, density, and task matters, and that
subjective and physiological measures can diverge.
Visual form also matters. Ahlström et al. (2010) showed that square menus can be faster than traditional hierarchical
menus because they exploit the full 2D space and reduce pointing time. Radial menus exploit direction and distance
in a different way, and Fitts’s law predicts that targets arranged around a central hub can be acquired quickly when
the direction is known (Fitts, 1954). The rounded-square icon grid and the radial lattice in this study are not arbitrary
novelties; they manipulate spatial geometry, visual salience, and motor trajectory, which are exactly the
dimensions that menu research says should matter.
1.2 Adaptive menus: benefits, costs, and the switching problem
Adaptive menus change item order, prominence, or availability based on usage. Findlater and McGrenere (2004)
compared static, adaptive, and adaptable menus and found that adaptation can help, but user control and
awareness moderate the benefit. Gajos et al. (2008) showed that predictability and accuracy trade off: an adaptive
interface that moves items can be faster in the moment but slower overall if users cannot predict where things are.
Lavie and Meyer (2010) catalogue the benefits and costs of adaptive user interfaces, including the cost of re-learning
and the risk of maladaptive changes. This is the core tension for the present project: a structure that fits the
current state may be better, but changing structure destroys spatial memory and imposes a transition cost.
The supplied RL paper (Gaspar-Figueiredo et al., 2025) is a useful counterpoint. It extends an RL-based adaptive-UI
framework with individual human feedback: each participant ranks pairs of adaptation videos, a personalised reward
modifier is trained, and a unique RL agent is trained for that participant. In a crossover study with 33 participants
across an e-learning and a trip-planning domain, the adaptive interface with human feedback produced higher user
satisfaction (estimated −0.97 for non-adaptive, p < .001) and higher engagement (−0.23, p = .016) than the non-
adaptive interface. The paper is important because it shows that individualised feedback can make adaptation
work, and because it carefully separates adaptive from non-adaptive baselines. But it adapts layout, font size,
density, theme, and widget type — not whole menu architectures — and it measures UX rather than product-finding

The fractal menu paper (Sahraoui et al., 2025) contributes a different idea: use the entire display as a recursive, self-
similar hierarchy. Each menu level is a rectangular area; selecting an area zooms into its children; selecting outside
the area zooms out; a breadcrumb supports direct return. The authors engineer the menu with an L-system, validate
it in a steel-mill checklist use case, and argue that self-similarity supports proprioceptive memory and orientation in
deep hierarchies. Crucially, the paper is a concept and engineering paper with a use-case validation, not a
controlled comparison. It explicitly says that empirical demonstration remains future work. The present study
provides that empirical test, and adds the emotional moderator the fractal paper does not consider.
1.3 Emotion, affect, and information seeking
Emotion is not a single variable. The most widely used descriptive model is the valence–arousal circumplex: affective
states vary in how pleasant they are and how activated they are (Russell, 1980). Mehrabian and Russell’s (1974) PAD
model adds dominance, which is relevant to navigation because perceived control is a core part of the experience.
Appraisal theories add cognitive structure: emotions arise from evaluations of goal relevance, goal congruence,
agency, and certainty (Ortony et al., 1988). This matters because frustration, confusion, and boredom are not just
points on a circle; they have different appraisals and different action tendencies.
Affect influences how people process information. Positive affect tends to broaden attention and increase
exploratory behaviour (Isen, 2001), which may make a visually richer or more exploratory menu more acceptable.
Negative affect narrows attention and increases vigilance, which may make a dense panel feel overwhelming. The
affect-as-information account (Schwarz & Clore, 1983) predicts that people may use their current feelings as a cue
when judging an interface, so the same menu can be evaluated differently depending on mood. In navigation
specifically, disorientation is a well-documented source of frustration (Lazar et al., 2003), and lostness has been
operationalised as the mismatch between the user’s expected path and the actual path (Smith, 1996). Boredom is the
mirror image: low arousal, low interest, and reduced exploration, which can lead to fast guessing or abandonment
(O’Brien et al., 2018).
Consumer research adds a domain-specific taxonomy. Richins’ (1997) Consumption Emotions Set and Laros and
Steenkamp’s (2005) hierarchical model distinguish positive and negative consumption emotions, including
contentment, excitement, frustration, and disappointment. The present study uses a smaller, experimentally tractable
set because repeated measurement of a large taxonomy is impractical. The four states are selected for their
theoretical distinctness, relevance to navigation, and measurability with brief self-report plus interaction
signals.

These predictions conflict in interesting ways. For example, under frustration, C is predicted to help because it reduces clicks, but it

1.6 Contributions
1. Empirical: the first 6 × 4 matrix of menu-structure performance and experience across experimentally induced emotional contexts,
with uncertainty estimates.
2. Causal: a test of whether emotion-matched structural switching beats a static best menu and a mismatched menu, separating
matching from mere change.
3. Methodological: a simulation-based power analysis for the 6 × 4 design, a menu-parity checklist, and a break-even accuracy analysis

4. Applied: a reusable open-source prototype and event schema for comparing heterogeneous menu architectures over one taxonomy.
5. Theoretical: evidence on whether the benefit of adaptation is explained by cognitive load, spatial memory, perceived control, or
visual engagement.

3.2 Why these two extra states — and why not anxiety or curiosity
The brief asked for two additional emotions beyond happiness and frustration. The recommendation is
confusion/lostness and boredom/disengagement. The rationale is that they are the two states most directly
implicated in navigation failure and in the engagement literature, and they occupy different quadrants of the affect
space:
Confusion is the cognitive-affective signature of wayfinding failure. It is the state that menu structure is most obviously supposed to
fix, and it is measurable through backtracking, category switching, and self-report.
Boredom is the low-arousal, low-engagement state that a visually rich or spatially novel menu might repair. It is the natural
counterpart to confusion: one is “I don’t know where it is,” the other is “I don’t care enough to keep looking.”
Together with happiness (positive, moderate arousal) and frustration (negative, high arousal), they cover the valence × arousal space
and add a motivational dimension that a pure circumplex misses.

3.3 Measurement model: self-report anchors, behavioural evidence, no mind-
reading
Each state is measured in three ways. First, self-report is the primary label: a 9-point SAM valence and arousal rating
(Bradley & Lang, 1994) plus four discrete 7-point items (happy, frustrated, confused, bored). Second, behavioural
signals are logged continuously and used as noisy features for the estimator. Third, manipulation checks verify that
the induction produced the intended state. The system never claims to detect emotion from behaviour alone; it
predicts the self-reported context under the tested conditions.
State labels are derived with a pre-registered rule. The intended state is the label if (a) the corresponding discrete
item is the highest of the four and (b) it is rated ≥ 5 on the 7-point scale. If the highest item is below 5 or two items
are tied, the trial is labelled mixed/neutral and analysed separately. This avoids forcing every participant into a
category and makes induction failure visible.
3.4 Induction protocol
All inductions are mild, short, and reversible. They are embedded before a block of six navigation trials, not during a
trial. A neutral reset (60-s nature video or a simple filler task) separates blocks. The order of blocks is
counterbalanced with a Latin square. The full scripts are in Appendix C.

Pilot criteria for a usable induction
An induction is retained only if, in the pilot (N = 8–10): (1) at least 60% of participants label the intended state as dominant; (2)
mean intensity is ≥ 5/7; (3) fewer than 20% report a different state as dominant; (4) no participant reports distress above 6/10; (5)
the induction takes ≤ 2.5 min including the check. If an induction fails, revise the script or replace it before the main study; do not
proceed with a weak manipulation.
3.5 Carry-over, reactivity, and demand characteristics
Emotional states can carry over between blocks, and repeated self-report can make participants aware of the
hypothesis. Three controls address this. First, a neutral reset and a 2-min break separate blocks. Second, the purpose
of the study is described broadly as “how people find products in different website layouts,” not as “we are testing
whether emotion changes menu choice.” Third, the post-experiment suspicion check asks what participants thought
the study was about; participants who correctly guess the adaptation hypothesis are retained but the analysis is
repeated without them as a sensitivity check.
Order effects are handled by counterbalancing the four emotion blocks across participants using a Latin square (four
sequences: ABCD, BCDA, CDAB, DABC). Within each block, the six menus are presented in a second Latin square (six
positions). The combination yields 24 sequences; participants are assigned randomly with stratification by gender
and prior online-shopping frequency. Trial position is included as a covariate in the analysis.
What this study does not claim
It does not diagnose emotion, measure clinical anxiety or depression, or infer emotion from facial expression. It does not claim
that the induced states are identical to real shopping emotions. It tests whether mild, experimentally induced, self-reported contexts
moderate the relative performance of menu structures. That is a narrower and more defensible claim.

The click-depth problem — state it, do not hide it
The mega menu (C) reaches a leaf in two clicks; the other five need three. That is a real advantage of mega menus and a plausible
reason frustrated users like them. But it means a C-vs-A difference cannot be attributed to “layout” alone. The analysis therefore
reports (a) the raw structural comparison, (b) a mediation analysis with clicks as the mediator, and (c) a matched-depth sensitivity
analysis on A/B/D/E/F only. If the C advantage disappears when clicks are equated, the paper says so. This is more honest than
adding a fake third click to C and pretending the menus are equal.

5.1 The fictional store
The store is called Meridian Timepieces (fictional; no real brand, logo, or trademark). It sells 72 watches across four
departments. Product images are either generated 3D renders or licensed stock images with brands removed; all
prices are fictional. The visual design is deliberately clean and consistent across conditions: white background, slate
text, teal accent, four department colours used as redundant cues. The same product detail page is used after a
correct selection in every menu, so the post-selection experience cannot explain differences.

5.2 Task design
The primary task is known-item product finding: the participant is given a short, natural-language shopping goal
and must reach the correct product detail page using the menu. The task never names the exact product; it
describes two or three attributes. This forces category navigation rather than string matching, while keeping the
target unambiguous.

the product attributes.

5.4 Event logging and data schema
Every interaction is logged as a JSON line with a client timestamp (performance.now()) and a server receipt
timestamp. The client timestamp is used for within-trial timing; the server timestamp is used for data integrity.
Events are batched every 500 ms and flushed on page unload; an offline queue prevents data loss if the connection
drops.
Field Type Description

Emotion block order: four sequences — ABCD, BCDA, CDAB, DABC — assigned evenly across participants. Each state appears in each
serial position exactly once across the four sequences.
Menu order within block: a 6 × 6 balanced Latin square, so each menu appears in each within-block position once. A different row is
used for each block to avoid the same menu always following the same state.

8.6 Trial procedure (exact sequence)
1. Task screen (5 s or until “Start”): the shopping goal is displayed in neutral language. The participant clicks “Start” when ready.
2. Navigation: the menu appears with the store home page. The participant uses the menu to reach the product. Search is visibly
disabled. The 120-s timer starts at “Start.”
3. Selection: clicking a leaf opens the product detail page. The system checks the product ID. If correct, the trial ends with a neutral
“Response recorded” message. If incorrect, the participant can continue; wrong leaves are logged but do not end the trial.
4. Timeout / give-up: at 120 s, or if the participant presses “I can’t find it,” the trial ends with “Time is up. Moving on.” No performance
feedback, praise, or criticism.
5. Inter-trial interval: 3 s blank screen with a fixation cross, then the next task.
6. Check-in: after trials 3 and 6 of each block, a 2-item micro-check (valence and arousal) appears; the full discrete-emotion check
appears at the end of the block. This limits reactivity while providing trial-level anchors.
Why neutral feedback? Praise or error messages would induce additional emotion and contaminate the block. The
product page itself is the success signal. The experimenter does not comment on performance during the session.

8.10 From matrix to policy
The output of Study 1 is not a single “best menu.” It is a 6 × 4 matrix with uncertainty. The policy is selected with a
pre-registered, transparent rule:
1. Guardrail: for each state, retain menus whose success rate is within 5 percentage points of the best menu in that state (non-inferiority
margin). If no menu is clearly best, use the global best menu for that state.
2. Efficiency: among retained menus, choose the one with the shortest geometric mean completion time.
3. Errors: if two menus are within 10% on time, choose the one with fewer navigation errors.
4. Experience: if still tied, choose the higher UMUX-Lite / lower NASA-TLX.
5. Uncertainty: require a posterior probability ≥ 0.90 that the chosen menu is best on the primary outcome, or a clear non-inferiority
result. Otherwise default to the global best menu and flag the state as “no reliable adaptation.”
6. Simplicity: if the chosen menu is within the guardrail of the global best, prefer the global best to avoid switching costs.
A Bayesian hierarchical model ( brms) with regularising priors on the 24 cell effects provides posterior probabilities
and shrinks extreme cell estimates. The frequentist models remain the primary inferential analysis; the Bayesian
model is used for policy selection and for communicating uncertainty. A utility-based sensitivity analysis (weights on
success, time, errors, usability) checks whether the policy changes under reasonable alternative weights.

9 Study 2 — causal test of adaptation
Study 1 can show that menus differ across states. It cannot show that using that mapping helps. Study 2
freezes the mapping and tests the causal claim against three counterfactuals: the best static menu, a
deliberately mismatched menu, and a sham switch that changes nothing but announces a change.
9.1 Objective and logic
The core comparison is matched vs static. If the matched condition is better, the mapping has applied value. But a
gain could come from mere change, novelty, or attention to the interface rather than from matching. The
mismatched condition controls for change without fit; the sham condition controls for the announcement of
change without any actual change. The four conditions form a 2 × 2 logic:
Condition Menu changes? Change is matched to state? What a gain would mean
Static No — Baseline: the best single menu.
Matched Yes Yes Fit + change. The target effect.
Mismatched Yes No Change without fit. If it also helps, the
effect is novelty/attention.
Sham No (banner only) — Announcement without change. If it
helps, the effect is demand/attention.

9.7 Analysis
The primary model is a mixed-effects model with condition, state, and condition × state as fixed effects, plus period,
sequence, and target set as covariates. Participant and target are crossed random intercepts. Planned contrasts are
matched vs static, matched vs mismatched, matched vs sham, and static vs mismatched. The matched-vs-static
success contrast is a non-inferiority test with a 5-percentage-point margin; time and errors are superiority tests.
Report the full condition × state matrix even if the interaction is not significant.
Carry-over: include period and sequence; test condition × period; if carry-over is present, analyse only the first period or use a
washout model.
Concordance: exclude concordant states from the matched-vs-static contrast; report the number of discordant states and the per-
state results.
Per-protocol: if a participant clicks “dislike” or does not attend to the transition, the trial remains in the ITT analysis; a per-protocol
analysis excludes trials with no detectable menu inspection.
Mediation: test whether the matched effect is mediated by switching cost, perceived disruption, cognitive load, and disorientation.
Use bootstrapped indirect effects with 5,000 resamples.
Equivalence: if matched and static are statistically indistinguishable, report an equivalence test against a pre-specified margin (5 pp
success; 10% time) rather than claiming “no difference.”
Bayesian: a hierarchical model with regularising priors provides the posterior probability that matched > static and that matched >
mismatched; use it for interpretation, not for p-hacking.

10 Study 3 — end-to-end system with an imperfect
estimator
Study 2 uses known, induced states. A real system must infer the state from noisy signals. Study 3 tests
whether the adaptation still helps when the state estimate is uncertain, and it identifies the accuracy at
which adaptation stops paying for itself.
10.1 Objective and design logic
The study compares a static condition (the global best menu) with a hybrid adaptive condition that uses a frozen
estimator, a confidence threshold, and a safe transition policy. The estimator is trained on Study 1 and validated with
participant-grouped cross-validation; it is not trained on Study 3 outcomes. The design is a within-subject crossover:
four emotion blocks, each with one static trial and one hybrid trial in counterbalanced order, plus a short free-
browsing task. This yields paired comparisons within each state while keeping the session under 75 minutes.

Model: a penalised multinomial logistic regression (L2, class-balanced) is the primary model because it is
interpretable and calibratable. Gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM) is a secondary model to check for non-linear
gains. The label is the four states plus a mixed class; the model outputs a probability distribution. Calibration is
checked with reliability diagrams and the Brier score; temperature scaling is fitted on held-out folds only.
Validation: leave-one-participant-out cross-validation on Study 1. Report macro-F1, balanced accuracy, per-class
recall, the confusion matrix, and calibration. Compare with a majority-class baseline and with an “oracle” model that
knows the induced state. The gap between the estimator and the oracle is the inference cost.
10.3 Transition policy
A prediction is not an instruction. The system changes the menu only when five conditions hold:
1. Confidence: the calibrated probability of the predicted state is ≥ 0.70 (tuned on Study 1, frozen before Study 3).
2. Expected gain: the predicted utility gain of the target menu over the current menu exceeds a pre-registered minimal meaningful gain
(e.g., 5% of the current expected utility).
3. Dwell: the current menu has been used for at least two trials, so the system does not oscillate.
4. Boundary: the switch happens between tasks, not mid-route.
5. User control: the banner offers “Keep current” and “Switch back”; a revert is logged and respected for the rest of the block.
If confidence is below threshold or the model sees an out-of-distribution feature pattern, the system keeps the
current menu and logs “no adaptation.” This fallback is a feature, not a failure: a system that abstains when uncertain
is safer than one that always switches.
10.4 Participants and sample size
N = 72 (full) or N = 24–48 (feasibility). The full sample gives ~80% power for a paired difference of d = 0.35 on the
hybrid-vs-static condition mean and a 95% CI half-width of about ±0.06 for a macro-F1 near .55 (assuming four
check-ins per participant). The reduced sample is a feasibility study, not a confirmatory estimator evaluation; report
precision and do not overclaim. Inclusion/exclusion are the same as Study 1, with no overlap.

11.2 Self-report timing and reactivity
Repeated self-report can interrupt navigation and make participants aware of their state. The design uses three
levels of measurement:
Block-level: full SAM + discrete items before and after each emotion block; NASA-TLX, UMUX-Lite, disorientation, and engagement
after each block. This is the primary self-report.
Micro-check: a 2-item valence/arousal check after trials 3 and 6 of each block (not after every trial). This provides trial-level anchors
without constant interruption.
End-of-session: SUS per menu, preference ranking, trust/adaptation items, suspicion check, and interview. These are not repeated per
trial.
For the estimator, the block-level label is the target; micro-checks are used only to assess fade. The model is trained
on Study 1 block labels and features from the preceding trials. This avoids using a trial-level self-report that would
be too sparse to train on.

12 Analysis plan
The analysis is pre-registered, model-based, and effect-size focused. The goal is not to find a significant
p-value for the 6 × 4 interaction; it is to estimate the matrix with enough precision to make a defensible
policy decision. Where the design is underpowered for a small effect, the paper reports the uncertainty
rather than pretending the null is definitive.
12.1 Principles
1. Pre-register the primary outcomes, the 6 × 4 interaction, the four focused contrasts, the Study 2 contrasts, and the policy rule.
2. Analyse as randomised (intention-to-treat) for the primary test; report per-protocol and CACE as sensitivity analyses.
3. Model the clustering of trials within participants and targets; do not treat 1,728 trials as independent.
4. Report effect sizes with 95% CIs; exact p-values; no dichotomous “significant / not significant” language without the estimate.
5. Correct for multiple comparisons within families; label exploratory analyses.
6. Freeze the mapping after Study 1; do not revise it after seeing Study 2.
7. Share the anonymised data, analysis code, task bank, and materials where ethics allow.

12.4 Multiplicity, equivalence, and the “no difference” problem
A non-significant interaction is not evidence that menus are equivalent. If the interaction is small and the CI is wide,
the correct conclusion is “inconclusive.” If the CI excludes a pre-specified meaningful effect, the correct conclusion is
“equivalent within the margin.” The paper therefore reports:
The interaction estimate and its 95% CI on the log-odds / log-time scale.
A equivalence test for the global-best menu vs the state-specific menu, with a margin of 5 percentage points on success and 10% on
time.
A “practical significance” statement: would the observed difference change the recommended menu under the policy rule?
12.5 Bayesian policy model
The policy model is a hierarchical Bayesian version of the primary model, fitted with brms. Regularising priors
(normal(0, 1) on the log-odds scale; half-normal(0, 1) on random-effect SDs) shrink extreme cell estimates and
produce posterior probabilities that each menu is best in each state. The policy rule in §8.10 is applied to the
posterior predictive distribution. The Bayesian model is not used to “find significance”; it is used to quantify
uncertainty and to avoid overfitting a 24-cell matrix with a modest sample.
12.6 Qualitative and process data
Retrospective think-aloud and interview data are analysed with reflexive thematic analysis. The codebook is
developed inductively from a subset of transcripts, then applied to the rest; a second coder independently codes
20% of the material. Disagreements are resolved by discussion. Qualitative data are used to explain unexpected
patterns, illustrate switching costs, and generate hypotheses; they are not used to replace the quantitative test.
Quotes are anonymised and checked for identifying details.

13 Sample size and power
The sample sizes in this plan come from a Monte Carlo simulation of the actual design, not from a rule
of thumb. The simulation shows something important: a 6 × 4 within-subject interaction has 15
numerator degrees of freedom, so it is much harder to detect than a two-condition comparison. The
plan is powered for a medium interaction, and it uses four focused contrasts to recover power for the
specific predictions.

14.4 Adaptation engine
The engine is deliberately conservative. It runs at task boundaries, uses a calibrated posterior, and abstains when
uncertain. The pseudocode below is the specification for Study 3; Study 2 uses a frozen policy with known labels and

prior = participant.blockCheckin             1-item state prior

state = argmax(posterior)

15 Ethics, privacy, and risk
The study induces mild emotions and observes navigation behaviour. Neither is inherently harmful, but
both require care: the frustration induction can feel manipulative, the confusion task can make
participants feel incompetent, and the interaction logs can become identifying if handled casually. The
ethics plan is therefore part of the design, not a formality at the end.
15.1 Approval and oversight
Obtain approval from the relevant institutional research ethics committee before recruitment or pilot data collection.
The protocol, consent forms, induction scripts, questionnaires, data-management plan, and debrief must be
approved. Any change to the induction, the deception, the data collected, or the sample requires an amendment. If
the study is conducted across institutions, obtain approval at each site and a data-sharing agreement.
15.2 Informed consent
Participants receive a plain-language information sheet covering: the purpose (comparing website layouts for
product finding); what they will do; the duration; the mild mood tasks; the fact that the interface may change; the
data collected (clicks, timing, questionnaire responses, optionally gaze); how data are stored and shared; the right to
withdraw without penalty; compensation; and contact details. Consent is recorded electronically or on paper,
separate from the research data.
Consent must mention the mood tasks without revealing the hypothesis
“This study compares different website navigation layouts. You will complete short product-finding tasks and brief mood exercises,
including a short comedy clip, a search task that may not work as expected, an ambiguous categorisation task, and a repetitive
catalogue task. These are mild and temporary. You can stop at any time.” This is honest about what happens without telling
participants that we are testing whether frustration changes menu choice.

15.4 Deception and debrief
The study uses mild deception: the “buggy search” is intentional, and the purpose is described broadly rather than as
an emotion-and-menu study. This is justified only if the deception is necessary (it is: knowing the hypothesis would
change behaviour), the risk is minimal, and the debrief is full. The debrief explains the four inductions, the menu
conditions, the adaptation hypotheses, and why the search failed. Participants can withdraw their data at that point
without penalty. The debrief also checks mood and offers a positive closing task.

15.6 Compensation and power
Compensate at £12–£15 per hour or course credit with a comparable alternative. Do not make compensation

for their time even if they withdraw. The experimenter is not the participant’s instructor or supervisor where possible;
if they are, a third party handles recruitment and consent to reduce power imbalance.
15.7 AI / estimator ethics
The estimator predicts a self-reported state; it does not diagnose a disorder, infer protected characteristics, or make
consequential decisions. It runs locally in the experiment and is not deployed to real users. The interface explains
when it adapts and why, offers “keep current” and “switch back,” and never changes mid-route. The paper reports
the estimator’s failure modes and does not claim emotional accuracy beyond the tested labels. If the system were
deployed commercially, it would need a separate fairness, transparency, and consent review.

A strong paper is not one that hides its weaknesses; it is one that anticipates them and designs around
them. This section states the threats to inference and the limits of what the study can claim. It also says
what evidence would falsify the core hypothesis.

16.2 Construct validity
Emotion labels: self-report measures the participant’s interpreted state, not a latent “true” emotion. The paper uses “self-reported
emotional context” and avoids clinical language.
Induction: a buggy search is a plausible frustration trigger, but it is not the same as real purchase frustration. The ambiguity task is a
proxy for category confusion, not for all confusion.
Menu constructs: “structure” is a bundle of geometry, breadth, depth, and persistence. The paper reports the bundle and uses
mediation/sensitivity to decompose it; it does not claim to isolate a single dimension.
Outcomes: success and time are objective but task-specific; usability and load are self-reported; engagement is inferred from self-
report and behaviour. No single measure is treated as the whole construct.
Questionnaires: SUS, UMUX-Lite, NASA-TLX, and SAM are established, but they were not designed for this exact context; pilot
reliability and comprehension checks are required.

16.4 Statistical conclusion validity
Power: the 6 × 4 omnibus is powered for medium effects; small effects will be inconclusive. The paper reports CIs and does not
interpret a non-significant interaction as proof of no effect.
Multiplicity: pre-registered families; Holm for focused contrasts; FDR for exploratory cells; all analyses labelled.
Model assumptions: random-effects structure, linearity on the log scale, distributional assumptions; diagnostics and sensitivity
models.
Missing data: failures are outcomes, not missing; questionnaire missingness handled with multiple imputation as a sensitivity analysis.
Researcher degrees of freedom: pre-registration, frozen analysis script, open data/code, no optional stopping.
Effect sizes: odds ratios, geometric mean ratios, standardized differences, and precision; practical significance tied to the policy rule.

Observation Conclusion
No menu × state interaction, and one menu is
best in every state.
Emotion-contingent structural adaptation is unnecessary in this domain. The paper
reports a robust global menu and discusses boundary conditions.
Interaction exists, but matched adaptation is
not better than the static best menu.
The cell-level differences are too small or the switching costs are too high.
Adaptation is notworth the disruption in practice.
Matched and mismatched both beat static by
the same amount.
The benefit is change/novelty/attention, not matching. The theoretical claim of
affective fit is not supported.
The estimator cannot beat the majority
baseline.
The interaction-signal approach is not viable with these features; the paper reports
the negative result and the features that would be needed.
Users systematically revert or report
disorientation.
Stability and control dominate performance; adaptation should be opt-in, not

The strongest version of the paper
The strongest version does not need every hypothesis to be confirmed. It needs a clean 6 × 4 matrix, a credible induction, a causal
test with the right counterfactuals, and an honest estimator evaluation. A paper that says “matching helps for frustration but not
for confusion, and the estimator is not yet accurate enough to deploy” is more useful than one that claims a universal win.
16.6 Limitations to state explicitly
1. The induced emotions are mild and temporary; they are not clinical states or necessarily the emotions of real shopping.
2. “Menu structure” is a bundle; the study cannot isolate every visual or motor dimension.
3. The mega menu has a click advantage; the analysis treats it as part of the structure and adds a matched-depth sensitivity analysis.
4. The lab setting and fictional store limit ecological validity; search is disabled in the main task.
5. The sample is not representative; cultural and age differences are not tested.
6. The estimator is trained on a small, lab-generated dataset; its accuracy is an upper bound on what a real deployment could achieve
without more data.
7. The study measures short-term adaptation; long-term learning, trust, and habituation are future work.

19 Conclusion
The project’s contribution is not “emotion-aware menus” as a slogan. It is a disciplined answer to three
questions: which menu structure fits which emotional context, whether using that mapping
causally helps, and how accurate the emotion estimate must be before adaptation is worth it.
The plan is deliberately conservative. It treats emotion as a self-reported context, not a hidden truth. It treats menu
structure as a bundle of geometry, breadth, depth, and persistence, and it measures the confounds rather than
pretending they do not exist. It uses a static best menu, a mismatched menu, and a sham switch as counterfactuals,
because “adaptive beats static” is not enough. It tests the estimator separately from the policy, because a good
mapping with a bad estimator can still fail. And it pre-registers what would count as failure.
If the result is positive, the paper offers a validated mapping, a causal estimate, and a deployable policy with a
confidence threshold. If the result is null, the paper still offers something useful: evidence that a stable menu is
robust to mild emotional context, or that switching costs dominate, or that the estimator is the bottleneck. Either
way, the study replaces a plausible intuition with evidence.

Appendix A — Task bank, difficulty rubric, and 24-task sample
A.1 Generation rules
1. Every leaf product receives a unique attribute vector: case size, strap, movement, water resistance, dial colour, and price band.
2. A task description uses two or three attributes and never the exact leaf label. It must map to exactly one product; the build-time
uniqueness test rejects ambiguous descriptions.
3. Tasks are written in natural shopping language (“You’re looking for…”), not menu language (“Go to Sport & Dive”).
4. Each task is assigned a difficulty level by the rubric below; the final bank is balanced 24 easy / 24 medium / 24 hard.
5. Each participant sees 24 tasks, balanced 8 / 8 / 8 across difficulty and across departments; the target set is rotated across participants.

Appendix B — Questionnaire items
B.1 Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM)
Valence: 9-point pictorial scale, 1 = very negative, 9 = very positive. Arousal: 9-point pictorial scale, 1 = very calm, 9 = very aroused.
Anchor: “How do you feel right now?”
B.2 Discrete emotion check
“Right now, how much do you feel each of the following?” 7-point scale (1 = not at all, 7 = extremely): happy, frustrated, confused,
bored. Add: “None of these describes how I feel” (checkbox). Dominant-state rule: the highest item must be ≥ 4 and at least 1 point
above the second-highest; otherwise the state is labelled mixed.
B.3 NASA-TLX workload (frustration subscale excluded)
Four items, 0–100: mental demand (“How mentally demanding was the task?”), temporal demand (“How hurried or rushed was the
pace?”), effort (“How hard did you have to work to accomplish your level of performance?”), performance (“How successful were you in
accomplishing what you were asked to do?”; reverse-scored). Report the mean or weighted score; report the standard frustration item
separately, never inside the workload composite.
B.4 UMUX-Lite
1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree. (1) “The interface’s capabilities meet my requirements.” (2) “The interface is easy to use.” Score:
((item1 − 1) + (item2 − 1)) / 12 × 100.
B.5 System Usability Scale (SUS)
10 items, 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree. Standard alternating positive/negative wording (Brooke, 1996). Score: odd items − 1; 5
− even items; sum × 2.5. Administer once per menu at the end of the session, with menu order counterbalanced.
B.6 Disorientation / control
7-point agreement: (1) “I felt lost.” (2) “I knew where I was in the store.” (3) “I could predict where to go next.” (4) “I felt in control of the
navigation.” Items 2–4 reverse-scored for a disorientation score; also report the four items separately.
B.7 Engagement (short form)
7-point agreement: (1) “I was absorbed in the task.” (2) “The interface was aesthetically appealing.” (3) “The task felt worthwhile.” (4) “I
wanted to continue exploring.” Adapted from O’Brien et al. (2018); report as a short engagement index, not as the full UES.
B.8 Trust / perceived intelligence (Study 2/3)
7-point agreement: (1) “The interface understood my needs.” (2) “The change was appropriate for the task.” (3) “I trusted the system.” (4)
“The interface felt intelligent.”
B.9 Adaptation experience (Study 2/3)
(1) “I noticed that the interface changed.” (2) “I understood why it changed.” (3) “The change disrupted my flow.” (4) “I would want this
adaptation in a real store.” (5) “I would prefer the previous layout.” Plus behavioural counts: keep-current clicks, switch-back clicks, dislike

Appendix C — Induction scripts
Happy (≈ 2.5 min). “For the next two minutes, please watch this short clip. It’s a light comedy scene; there’s nothing you need
to do except watch. You can look away or stop if you prefer.” [Play the clip.] “Thanks. Take a moment. How do you feel right
now?” [SAM + discrete check.]
Frustration (≈ 2.5 min). “Please use the search box to find the ‘Titanium Deep’ watch. The search is still being tested, so it may
be slow.” [The search fails with a generic “No results — try again” message.] “Please try once more.” [Second failure.] “One more
try, please.” [Third failure.] “Thank you — that’s enough for this part. The search was designed to fail; it wasn’t your fault.”
[Record frustration; offer a 30-s neutral pause before the trials.]
Confusion (≈ 2.5 min). “Here are six watches. Please sort them into the two categories you think the store uses. There’s no
right answer — we’re interested in how people make sense of ambiguous categories. Talk me through your thinking as you
go.” [The items are deliberately ambiguous: two could fit either category; no feedback is given.] “Thanks. That was intentionally
ambiguous; the ambiguity is the point of this part.”
Boredom (≈ 90 s). “Please check the price of each watch on this list and write it on the sheet. It’s a routine data-entry task;
accuracy matters more than speed.” [20 near-identical rows; no feedback; repeat until 90 s.] “Thanks — that’s the end of the
data-entry task.”
Neutral reset (≈ 60 s). “Let’s take a short pause. Please watch the screen; there’s nothing to remember or do.” [Play 60 s of
calm nature footage, no abrupt cuts or loud audio.] “When you’re ready, we’ll continue.”
Stop rules
Stop the induction immediately if the participant says “stop,” rates distress > 6/10, shows signs of panic or tearfulness, or asks to
leave. Stay with them, offer water and a neutral activity, and debrief. Record the event and report it to the ethics committee. Never
continue a block “to complete the data.”

Appendix H — Consent and debrief outline

Purpose: compare website navigation layouts for finding products.
What you will do: product-finding tasks, short mood exercises (a comedy clip, a search task that may not work, an ambiguous

Time: about 90 minutes (Study 1), 65 minutes (Study 2), 70 minutes (Study 3).

Risks: mild, temporary frustration, confusion, or boredom; you can stop at any time.

H.2 Debrief script
“Thank you for taking part. I can now explain the full purpose. We are comparing six different menu layouts and asking whether
the best layout depends on how someone feels. The search task earlier was designed to fail — that was part of the study, not

Appendix I — Glossary
Term Definition in this plan
Adaptive navigation A system that changes the menu structure, content, or presentation in response to the user’s state or
behaviour.
Affective fit The hypothesis that a menu’s structural demands should match the processing style associated with the
current emotional context.
Breadth The number of options visible at a given menu level.
Depth The number of levels or clicks required to reach a leaf.
Fractal menu A recursive layout in which the same spatial pattern repeats at each level; selecting a parent zooms into its
children.
Mega menu A wide panel that shows multiple subcategories and leaves at once, usually two clicks from the top level.
Radial lattice A menu arranged around a centre, with options at compass sectors and concentric rings.
RS grid A grid of large rounded-square icon cards, colour-coded by category.
State label The dominant self-reported emotion after induction, or mixed if no item dominates.
Matched condition The menu selected by the Study 1 policy for the current state.
Mismatched condition A pre-registered derangement of the state→menu mapping; controls for change without fit.
Sham condition A banner/announcement of change with no actual layout change; controls for demand and attention.
Break-even accuracy The estimator accuracy at which the expected utility of hybrid adaptation equals that of the static best
menu.
