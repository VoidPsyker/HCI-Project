Eyes Bring out Effectiveness? Applying Eye-Tracking to Explore 
Consumers’ Visual Perception of Horizontal and Vertical Menu Formats 
in E-commerce Website Design
1 INTRODUCTION
In recent years, scholars have initiated inquiries into website menus (Naylor, 2016; Ouyang & Zhou, 2018), proposing 
that the fundamental design of menus should underscore both breadth and depth (Naylor, 2016). With technological 
progress, menus have undergone alterations in diverse presentation formats. In a prior investigation into medical website 
design, it was determined that the quantity of screens necessary to navigate to the desired content exerted the most 
significant influence on usability (Rotondi et al., 2021). Every website necessitates a rapid and effective navigation 
system(Leuthold et al., 2011), as it is crucial for ensuring that users can easily and efficiently find the information they are 
looking for, thereby enhancing their overall experience and satisfaction.. Many books on website or user interface design 
broadly categorize navigation systems into two consecutive conceptual tasks (Deaton, 2003; Garrett, 2022; Kalbach, 2007; 
Lynch et al., 1999; Rosenfeld & Morville, 2002; Shneiderman & Plaisant, 2010): (1) the task of organizing items into 
meaningful clusters and (2) the task of tour design, which involves determining how to present the problem of linking with 
other tour mechanisms to encourage users to navigate as efficiently and effectively as possible. Hence, the efficacy of the 
navigation system plays an exceedingly important role on the website. Based on Just and Carpenter's eye-mind hypothesis 
(Just & Carpenter, 1976), this study investigates perception and cognition, dividing them into distinct components. Eye 
attention is a crucial design principle for attracting people's focus(Cuddihy & Spyridakis, 2012). In this study, Eye-tracking 
gaze patterns provide primary data on users' visual movement and efficiently, while real-time user feedback during 
operation offers secondary insights into immediate user cognition and overall effectively. A review of relevant literature 
reveals that previous menu studies have predominantly focused on user interface design and user search behavior across 
different task types, with limited in-depth exploration into perception and cognition of individual menu systems. Therefore, 
the core of this study is to propose recommendations based on eye-tracking dynamics for user perception of mega menus.
A well-designed navigation system not only resolves the issue of getting lost promptly but also enhances information-
seeking efficiency. While the Internet continues to progress rapidly (Zheng, 2015), there remains a significant number of 
websites employing ladder menus, also known as drop-down menus (Naylor, 2016). Consequently, recent research in 
navigation systems has primarily focused on delving deeper into drop-down menus. Over the years, a diverse range of 
menu types has been investigated (Hornof & Halverson, 2003; Kiger, 1984; Kreigh et al., 1990; Landauer, 1987; Naylor, 
2016; Zaphiris, 2000). However, the relatively new mega menu has received less attention in research endeavors. Several 
studies in the existing literature have investigated menu design in connection with mega menus. For instance, in Hornof 
and Halverson's study, it was shown that the label menu and the mega menu are similar in terms of presentation formats. 
Their findings demonstrated that the labeled layout resulted in faster search speeds compared to the unlabeled layout. 
However, it is worth noting that the focus of Hornof and Halverson's study was primarily on search strategies and 
categorization/tagging rather than the intricacies of menu design itself (Hornof & Halverson, 2003). In 2011, Leuthold

In the domain of human-computer interaction (HCI), substantial research and scrutiny into the aesthetics of user 
interfaces gained momentum following the seminal study by Kurosu and Kashimura in 1995(Kurosu & Kashimura, 1995; 
Tuch et al., 2012). Particularly within the realm of user experience (UX) research, the aesthetics of user interfaces has 
emerged as a prominently discussed subject(Bargas-Avila & Hornbæk, 2011; Tuch et al., 2012). Despite numerous studies 
highlighting a significant correlation between usability and aesthetics(Hartmann et al., 2008; Lavie & Tractinsky, 2004; 
Quinn & Tran, 2010; Tractinsky et al., 2000), historical research findings still exist suggesting that aesthetics does not 
significantly impact usability (Hassenzahl, 2004; Van Schaik & Ling, 2009). As a result, recent studies have indicated that 
the presence of usability elements could potentially influence users' perceptions of a website's aesthetics. Furthermore, this 
notion may extend to the concept that functionality and aesthetic appeal are closely intertwined, suggesting that effective 
functionality is inherently aesthetically pleasing. Research on the relationship between usability and aesthetics has recently 
gained traction in empirical studies. Ben-Bassat et al. (2006) and Lee and Koubek (2010) have highlighted the significant 
impact of interface usability on users' perceptions of aesthetics. Their findings suggest that interfaces that are easier to use 
tend to be perceived as more aesthetically pleasing (Ben-Bassat et al., 2006; Lee & Koubek, 2010). While previous research 
has hinted at the notion that "beautiful things are easier to use," this idea is only partially supported by empirical evidence. 
More recent scholars have started to posit that usability might exert a more direct influence on user interface evaluation 
than aesthetics do, shaping users' overall perceptions of the interface. Therefore, while UI concentrates on the overall 
presentation of the interface and holds significant importance, UX encompasses broader aspects such as system 
accessibility, efficiency, and user enjoyment

Information Architecture (IA) navigation system consist of links to various pages, and the arrangement of these linked 
pages is commonly termed as a mini-IA, significantly influencing usability(Naylor, 2016). While menus represent just a

In 2011, Leuthold, Schmutz, and other researchers utilized eye-tracking technology to investigate disparities in visual

cognition(Leuthold et al., 2011). Presently, the scope of menu studies utilizing eye-tracking devices lacks diversity, 
necessitating exploration of additional menu types for related research. As asserted by Jacob and Karn (2003) and supported 
by Nielsen and Pernice (2010), eye-tracking represents a potent method for delving into navigation design intricacies, 
facilitating comprehension of how users' eye movements and saccade patterns are deployed for information acquisition.

manageable bounds for experimental efficacy, affords researchers deeper insights into individuals' behaviors throughout 
the information retrieval process (Jacob & Karn, 2003; Nielsen & Pernice, 2010). Hence, drawing upon prior research and 
literature, this study discerned that amalgamating both horizontal and vertical configurations within the mega menu 
structure could notably amplify users' effectiveness in Known-item seeking information retrieval. Historically, eye-
tracking studies have often depicted users' overall scanning patterns resembling the F-shaped (or E-, Z-shaped) when 
navigating web pages(Nielsen, 2006; Nielsen Norman Group, 2009; Shrestha & Owens, 2009). This investigation seeks to 
elucidate whether aligning menu formats (i.e., vertical versus horizontal) with human oculo-physiological scanning 
tendencies constitutes a contributory factor in augmenting user efficiency. The F-shaped layout indicates that the primary 
menu is displayed horizontally, while the secondary menu is displayed vertically. Diverging from traditional horizontal or 
vertical layouts, this study aims to integrate the F-shaped concept into the visual breakpoints within the user experience 
flow of menus. By introducing breakpoints, the overall efficiency of user interactions is thereby enhanced. 
In the relevant domain, it was found that in 1988, scholars conducted a study involving six types of menu layouts, 
ultimately demonstrating that a two-level menu structure could achieve optimal operational performance (Parkinson et al., 
1988).  This optimal menu prototype bears resemblance to today's mega menu layout. However, in 2016, Naylor posited 
that the type of menu does not affect search performance, a finding inconsistent with previous research (Naylor, 2016). 
Nevertheless, upon reviewing past studies, there has been only preliminary discussion on mega menus, with most 
experimental research relying on the duration of time to assess efficiency. Direct validation and discussion on the efficiency 
of menus at higher levels remain scarce. Therefore, there exists untapped potential and scope for expansion in this field.

that 'we cannot ask users to take the time to learn to understand our information architecture, so we should do our best to

al., 2015; Rosenfeld & Morville, 2002). IA is an extension of Typology(Rosenfeld & Morville, 2002; Wodtke, 2011; 
Wurman & Saul, 1996), which is composed of three main structures, namely context, content, and navigation. In 1997, IA

In 2017, scholars mentioned that IA is an important component of a website and that IA in a website significantly 
affects its usability and user experience during navigation(Rosenfeld & Morville, 2002; Schmitt, 1999; Wan Mohd Isa et 
al., 2007). It is even suggested that IA is not just a theory, but a strategy for a website as well as an application of best 
practices. IA is the result of continuous iteration and negotiation between website designers and administrators (Dijck, 
2003; Ruzza et al., 2017; Schmitt, 1999). In 2020, scholars emphasized the significance of information architecture within 
websites and recommended that it be conscientiously integrated into website navigation systems during the development 
phase (Tang et al., 2020). IA can be likened to a tree; with a solid foundation, the overall experience becomes more stable 
and reassuring. Consequently, numerous scholars have emphasized the substantial influence of a well-crafted IA on the 
overall user experience. In a navigation system, a menu represents a set of pages interconnected through a series of links. 
This interconnected structure of linked pages is commonly termed as a mini-information architecture, exerting significant 
influence on the usability of each touchpoint (Naylor, 2016). While the menu constitutes a subset of the overarching 
information architecture, it functions as a microcosm thereof, encapsulating the primary data structure of the website and 
facilitating rapid information retrieval for users via the menu interface. 
The book categorizes users’ behaviors into two dimensions: integration and iteration and also outlines the four most 
common ways of finding information, including exhaustive research, exploratory seeking, refinding, and known-item 
seeking (Rosenfeld & Morville, 2002). Known-item seeking was initially introduced in the context of library materials 
seeking and is defined as the assumption that the searcher knows the information and is looking for a specific item 
(Wildemuth & O'Neill, 1995). In a 1989 study, the concepts of "known-item search" and "closed task" were investigated. 
The study highlighted that the time required for an intentional information search was shorter compared to a random or 
purposeless search. This phenomenon stems from the fact that focused tasks provide individuals with a clearer objective 
for information retrieval. Conversely, aimless searches necessitate more time as they involve sifting through a broader 
range of information (Marchionini, 1989). In contrast, a study in 1993 revealed that individuals engaging in Known-item 
seeking were more inclined to employ personalized analyses and strategies during their searches (Qiu, 1993). Synthesizing 
the aforementioned literature, Known-item seeking not only manages task duration, enhancing research efficiency, but also 
illuminates the behavioral characteristics inherent in individuals during information retrieval processes. Thus,this research 
aims to understand the user visual perception of known-item seeking behavior on information architecture of menu design.
Numerous theoretical concepts and definitions concerning information-seeking behavior have been examined in 
previous research. In 2003, scholars presented a white paper addressing four major areas critical to constructing 
comprehensive website information: Knowledge elicitation, Organization and structure of information, Retrieval of 
information, and Presentation of information (Proctor et al., 2003). Understanding users' expectations from a website 
during information-seeking processes can significantly enhance website satisfaction (Ping et al., 1999). Therefore, this 
study expands upon and delves into user-centered research to achieve a more profound understanding of website 
information architecture from the perspectives of both users and experts.

2.1.1 Navigation System: Information Seeking
A navigation system plays a pivotal role in improving the usability of a website, a task inherently laden with cognitive 
demands for users. According to Jul (2004), a navigation system comprises four cognitive subtasks: information gathering, 
wayfinding, spatial knowledge acquisition, and mobility(Jul, 2004). In a web environment, when users struggle to locate 
desired content, they often experience a loss of orientation and become disoriented within the information space (Chang et 
al., 2022; Lazar et al., 2003; Levene, 2011; Nah & Davis, 2002), leading to user frustration (Lazar et al., 2003). This 
frustration, in turn, impacts the website's brand image, increasing the likelihood of users seeking alternative websites (Krug, 
2000; Naylor, 2016; Rosenfeld & Morville, 2002) for their information needs. Hence, throughout the website development 
phase, strategic considerations must be given to augmenting user engagement, encouraging purchasing activities, and 
fostering repeat visits, thereby bolstering the website’s overall integrity and commercial viability (Naylor, 2016; Nielsen, 
1999). Besides underscoring the significance of user interface design (Tractinsky et al., 2000), a comprehensive approach 
to evaluating the website's interactivity is crucial to sustain user retention, ensuring the website’s enduring functionality 
and efficacy over time.
Throughout the literature, diverse perspectives have been presented by scholars regarding information-seeking behavior. 
In 1989, Marcia Bates introduced the "Berry-picking" model, which elucidates how users formulate information needs at 
the outset of their search process and subsequently develop search parameters based on these needs. As the search unfolds 
along intricate pathways, users progressively traverse these paths to access additional information (akin to picking 
strawberries). Consequently, users gradually refine their understanding of their needs and modify their initial search criteria 
(Bates, 1989). This perspective aligns with assertions made by scholars in 2015, who propose that navigation within the 
information landscape is guided by individual conceptual frameworks. Users determine their subsequent actions based on 
task objectives and contextual elements (Jul & Furnas, 1997; Zheng, 2015). In the navigation system, users can manifest a 
higher degree of personal awareness in their information-seeking behavior. Users engaging in information seeking can be 
classified into three primary groups: Low information seekers, High information seekers, and Selective information seekers. 
Investigating the group of selective information seekers may reveal a greater degree of homogeneity compared to the high 
or low information seeker groups.
Fenichel’s (1981) research examined search performance within an online database system, revealing a noteworthy 
correlation between users’ familiarity with a website and their search efficacy (Fenichel, 1981; Kim, 2001). Moreover, 
users’ experiences during searches significantly influence their adopted search strategies (Kim, 2001). Synthesizing these 
prior investigations, it becomes evident that navigation systems intricately intertwine with users’ behaviors throughout 
information retrieval, consequently impacting their overall experience, search strategies, and efficiency. In essence, extant 
literature underscores the pivotal role of navigation systems within information architectures, not only facilitating users’ 
access to information but also steering them towards optimal exploration within the digital information domain. A well-
designed navigation system not only resolves the issue of getting lost promptly but also enhances information-seeking 
efficiency. Common navigation tools encompass menus, website maps, and navigation paths (Zheng, 2015). Despite the 
increased emphasis on search systems in recent years, navigation systems remain fundamental and are often deemed the 
"last mile" for users in accessing valuable information. Presenting website content and information structure in a 
straightforward format (Danielson, 2002; Tedesco et al., 2008; Zhang & Salvendy, 2001; Zheng, 2015) constitutes one of 
the pivotal factors contributing to a website's usability (Palmer, 2002; Zheng, 2015).

2.1.2 Menu Format
Menus, as a common method of website navigation, encompass a collection of functions interlinked with various pages. 
The structural organization of these page links, often denoted as "information architecture," significantly influences the 
usability of a website (Naylor, 2016). In 2015, Jack G. Zheng assessed navigation systems using a two-dimensional 
framework, addressing both information structure and usability. This evaluation provided an exhaustive examination of 
primary navigation tools and systems then in use, thereby offering a comprehensive analysis of menus within navigation 
systems alongside an in-depth exploration of IA and usability. Zheng's work contributed to a more refined understanding, 
description, and conceptualization of menus within navigation frameworks.
In the past, the most commonly used type of menu was referred to as a Global Menu or Global Navigation, and it served 
as one of the primary menu styles in navigation systems. Typically, this menu is displayed horizontally at the top of a 
webpage or vertically on its left side. Global menus primarily provide links to the top-level pages of a website, with sub-
level options appearing as needed (Zaphiris et al., 2002; Zheng, 2015), adhering to fundamental principles of sequential 
menu design. However, global menus that display only select top-level links no longer align with contemporary trends. 
Scholars have emphasized the importance of menus containing more information-structured content. Therefore, menus 
incorporating multi-level structured content are termed Multi-level navigation menus or Structure Preview Designs (Zhang 
& Salvendy, 2001). In addition to the drop-down menu, which is the most common menu in the past few years, a variety 
of menu forms have begun to appear, such as fly-out menu (used for vertical menus on the left or right side), pop-up menu 
(at the bottom of the page), multi-level navigation bar, mega menu, fat footer, HTML sitemap page, breadcrumbs and so 
on. As such, this study compiles recent scholarly definitions of 'menu' and presents them in Table 1.

2.2 User Experience (UX)
In 2006, Hassenzahl provided a comprehensive overview and analysis of user experience, illustrating that it extends 
beyond mere technique to embody a subjective, contextualized, intricate, and dynamic encounter (Hassenzahl & Tractinsky, 
2006). Google defines UX as a phenomenon where empathy assumes a pivotal role, and it emphasizes that UX consists of 
usable, equitable, enjoyable, and useful attributes, all of which significantly impact both customers and businesses. Don 
Norman and Nielsen in 2016 provided a definition of user experience (UX) as the foremost criterion for fulfilling users' 
actual needs. This entails a comprehensive service design approach spanning various domains, including engineering, 
operations, graphics, industrial design, and interface design, etc. The study emphasizes the critical distinction between user 
experience (UX) and user interface (UI). While UI concentrates on the overall presentation of the interface and holds 
significant importance, UX encompasses broader aspects such as system accessibility, efficiency, and user enjoyment, etc. 
This differentiation underscores the necessity of separately addressing UX and UI for a holistic design approach. The 
usability of a website pertains to the procedural aspects that a user engages with while navigating a website.  (Norman & 
Nielsen, 2016).

enhance the human-machine interface(Nielsen, 1994a, 1994b, 2012; Nielsen & Levy, 1994; Nielsen & Pernice, 2010). 
Nielsen introduced fundamental concepts in usability engineering in 1994 and 2012, collaborating with Levy (1994) and 
Pernice (2010) on related research and developments. According to Webster and Ahuja (2006), the organizational structure

2.3 Eye-Tracking
The reason why research efforts have not burgeoned despite significant improvements in eye-tracking technology over 
the years is clearly explained in a study conducted in 2003. This limitation can be attributed to the necessity of individually 
calibrating eye-tracking instruments for each subject, coupled with the fact that approximately 10-20% of subjects may 
evade successful tracking. Moreover, a substantial amount of time is dedicated to the analysis and organization of data, 
with uncertainties persisting in data interpretation. For instance, discerning whether prolonged or frequent fixation on a 
subject by viewers signifies its importance or its complexity remains ambiguous (Jacob & Karn, 2003). In recent years, 
due to technological advancements, research in the field of eye tracking has proliferated. Recent studies indicate that during 
website browsing, people's visual attention typically conforms to an F-shape pattern (although some scholars have also 
proposed E- or Z-shape patterns) when scanning the entire website (Nielsen, 2006; Nielsen Norman Group, 2009; Shrestha 
& Owens, 2009). Moreover, scholars have posited that viewing priority typically begins at the top left corner, progresses 
to the top right corner, then moves to the bottom left corner, and finally to the bottom right corner. It has also been suggested 
that viewing behavior proceeds horizontally from left to right, followed by a vertical movement from top to bottom 
(Djamasbi et al., 2011; Moran, 2020). From an ocular perspective, the horizontal trajectory exhibits greater regularity 
compared to the vertical trajectory (Goldberg et al., 2002; Pan et al., 2004).
Namoun indicated in 2018 highlighted that when an object (task) is positioned within a fixed area, it heightens the 
concentration of attention during observation, consequently resulting in extensive coverage of the area of interest by eye-
tracking, a phenomenon termed as depth scanning mode. The textual layout appears to influence the trajectory of the user's 
gaze during viewing, with a columnar layout being more aesthetically pleasing than a grid layout. Nonetheless, prior 
research has only tentatively suggested that a column layout garners visual attention, and its impact on information search 
efficiency remains inconclusive (Namoun, 2018). According to the "eye-mind" hypothesis proposed by Just and Carpenter,

when an individual fixates on a particular passage of text or a specific region, the brain concurrently processes the 
corresponding information (Just & Carpenter, 1976). Put differently, eye movement data can furnish a dynamic trace of 
the locus of a person's attention within a visual display during observation (Hung & Wang, 2021). 
In 2011, Leuthold, Schmutz, et al. employed eye-tracking devices to investigate disparities in users' visual perceptions 
among simple menus, dynamic menus, and vertical menus. The study's findings indicated that vertical menus require users 
to glance less frequently, operate more swiftly, and achieve a higher success rate. Consequently, this research posits that 
vertical menus are more conducive to exploring user perception and cognition (Leuthold et al., 2011). Nevertheless, the 
scope of eye-tracking applications in menu studies remains somewhat limited, underscoring the necessity for future studies 
to encompass a broader array of menu types. In 2003, Jacob and Karn highlighted the burgeoning interest in eye-tracking 
research within the domain of human-computer interaction, particularly its relevance to investigating user perceptions 
during menu item search processes. Their study synthesized findings from 20 usability studies, revealing a transition of 
focus from traditional computer menu navigation towards web search activities and information extraction from web pages. 
This evolution included a shift from targeted item searches within menus to exploratory browsing behaviors across websites 
without predefined objectives(Jacob & Karn, 2003). Jacob and Karn's insights underscore the utility of eye-tracking studies 
in informing navigation design and illuminating the role of eye gaze in information retrieval processes(Jacob & Karn, 
2003; Nielsen & Pernice, 2010).
2.4 Phase Summary
Summarizing the foregoing literature review, this study aims to extend the future directions and address the gaps 
identified by previous scholars. First, it underscores the significance of the menu within the navigation system as a pivotal 
determinant of website usability. Second, it highlights the varying outcomes observed in prior research regarding the 
efficacy of expansive menus in facilitating user search processes, underscoring the need for more comprehensive data 
analysis in this regard. Thirdly, it advocates for a broader application of eye-tracking technology in menu research 
encompassing diverse menu types. Fourth, it advocates for the further expansion of eye-tracking utilization. Specifically, 
the integration of eye-tracking devices in menu research should encompass a wider spectrum of applications. Historically, 
empirical investigations have identified Eye-tracking Measurement Methods (EMMs) as effective tools for probing user 
search behavior and gaze patterns within navigation systems structured by information architecture (Nielsen & Pernice, 
2010). Such methodologies enable profound analysis of physiological data, thereby enhancing researchers' comprehension 
of user behaviors. Furthermore, eye-tracking technology plays a pivotal role in experimental settings, facilitating post-
experimentation recall through the playback of eye movements and supporting semi-structured interviews (Bartels, 2019).
This study commences by providing an initial clarification of the menu hierarchy, drawing upon Kalbach's (2007) 
conceptualization of the hierarchical organization of a website (Kalbach, 2007). Within this framework, the elements 
comprising the hierarchical structure are denoted as "Hierarchical Structure," with the uppermost tier designated as "Main 
Navigation," and subsequent tiers referred to as "Local Navigation." Thus, in alignment with Kalbach's hierarchical model, 
this study adopts the terminology of "Main Menu" for the primary level menu and "Local Menu" for the subordinate levels.

5 FINDINGS SUMMARY
After adjusting to the feedback from users and experts, the update has been effective in improving the efficiency of 
users in ordering meals. In Study 1, we found that user feedback was more concerned with the complexity of the menu 
categories and the overall experience. After discussing with experts, we constructed an idea that "UI is the first step to 
attract users, and UX is the second step to make a good impression. With both UI and UX strategies, we can drive customers 
back." Therefore, through user feedback and previous eye-tracking results, we found that users are good at viewing flat 
surfaces in an F-shape. Combining visual feedback and previous objective eye movement behaviour results, we ultimately 
recommended that the main menu and local menu to be presented in a combination of horizontal and vertical formats in 
the Study One. The results of Study One provided a good basis for a follow-up eye-tracking study, and through feedback 
from experts and users, we iterated on a second version of the (horizontally-integrated-vertical) design.
In Study 2, we divided the discussion into two parts: the main menu and the local menu. For the main menu, the result 
showed that too much detail in the menu would increase the time required for users to understand and guess, which may 
cause uncertainty in users' search for information. In the second version of the main menu, we simplified the layout of the 
menu that helped improve users' comprehension and viewing efficiency, according to the study. As for the local menu, it 
was found that the original version of the menu did not have a significant effect on usability and satisfaction; however, in 
the updated version of the menu, the local menu improved the efficiency (Effective Focus) of the users in finding 
information.
The findings suggest that employing straightforward and succinct menu designs for main menus effectively enhances 
users’ attention. Increased attention on main menus correlates with a higher likelihood of subsequent exploration, including

6CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION
In this study, mega menus are defined as those that can display lower-level links within the same screen area, providing 
a clear view of the entire menu structure. This presentation of both breadth and depth enables users to efficiently navigate 
directly to their desired targets, bypassing sequential selections. Drawing upon previous scholarly concepts of hierarchy 
(Kalbach, 2007), this research explores both main menus and local menus.
Therefore, it is important to recognize that excessive reliance on local menu may result in diminished user usability and 
satisfaction with a website. This study introduces a novel perspective on user dynamics within a navigation system. This 
approach adapts the traditional flat F-shaped heat map model to the user experience viewing line and establishes visual 
breakpoints through horizontal and vertical integration, thereby enhancing operational efficiency within the user 
experience. Menus represent a prevalent method for website navigation, comprising a series of links to various pages that 
collectively form the website’s information structure, significantly influencing its usability (Naylor, 2016). Leuthold et al. 
(2011) note that vertical menus lead to reduced gaze frequency, faster navigation speeds, and higher success rates, 
underscoring their suitability for perception and cognition(Leuthold et al., 2011). Future research endeavors should 
encompass diverse navigation systems to tackle the multifaceted challenges confronting present and future users in 
navigation tasks. When confronted with intricate tasks, users tend to adapt navigation strategies (Leuthold et al., 2011) and 
tailor their behavior according to their own experience or the information acquired during information retrieval (Jul & 
Furnas, 1997; Zheng, 2015). Fenichel (1981) was one of the earliest to indicate that users' website search experience 
influences their search performance (Fenichel, 1981; Kim, 2001). Hence, building upon previous research, this study 
centers on enhancing user experience efficiency through menu design to alleviate users' cognitive processing load during 
information retrieval. Previous studies have frequently discussed the phenomenon of individuals following F or Z-shaped 
trajectories during visual perception, primarily within static planar viewing contexts. This study extends the concept of F-
shaped trajectories to dynamic operations through experimental manipulation. In the design of the main menu, a horizontal 
layout is more effective in capturing viewers’ attention, while for the local menu, a vertical F-shaped layout is integrated 
into the design considerations. As anticipated, the second version of the local menu presented in a vertical format 
demonstrated superior visual efficiency. This outcome reinforces the critical role of UI design in initial user engagement, 
with UX being paramount in user retention.
This study synthesizes input from both users and experts, providing a foundational framework for subsequent research 
on the validation of Version 2. Initial heat map analysis clearly reveals that participants exhibited enhanced eye focus when 
presented with a vertically oriented local menu. Additionally, our investigation found a marked decrease in concentration 
at the first level of the main menu, in contrast to the engagement elicited by static images. Considering the concept of 
'Known-item seeking,' users typically seek information in the most efficient and expedient manner possible to facilitate 
decision-making. We aim to reduce the time spent in the search process overall. Version 2 of the platform demonstrated 
enhanced efficiency in viewing and proved to be more effective than the multi-category format, as indicated by statistical

caused confusion among respondents, leading to increased navigation time and feelings of confusion. Concerning local 
menu, users tend to access them more frequently when they find the content of the main menu intriguing. Nevertheless, 
when users navigate these local menus to seek information, the efficiency of the menu interface plays a crucial role. 
Investing an appropriate amount of time in navigating local menu can enhance usability and user satisfaction. Conversely, 
prolonged search times may diminish the user’s perception of the site’s usability and satisfaction levels. Skillful design of 
local menu further enhances user efficiency, subsequently contributing to the overall smoothness of site navigation (refer 
to Table 7).
Based on prior research, this study investigates the contributions of mega-menus to navigation systems within 
information architecture, delineated as follows: (1) a comprehensive assessment of the efficacy of mega-menus in 
enhancing users' search efficiency, utilizing eye-tracking data; (2) a more thorough and multifaceted exploration within 
the realm of menu design; (3) precise delineations of each menu type; and (4) the integration of the visual trajectory  (F-
shaped) of the former flat plane into the dynamic operation process to present the main and local menus both horizontally 
and vertically. Furthermore, the study underscores the significance of usability within navigation systems (Palmer, 2002; 
Zheng, 2015). Despite the technological boom in the field of human-computer interaction, comprehensive research on 
menus remains scarce. While most users rely on search systems to locate information, menus serve as navigational aids 
akin to maps. In situations where search systems are unavailable, navigation systems become crucial tools for information 
retrieval. Despite being a passive tool, navigation systems are indispensable, enabling users to navigate websites with 
confidence. Even in the event of search system failure, navigation systems offer a dependable alternative.
"Is Beautiful Design Correlated with Enhanced Usability?" (Tractinsky et al., 2000). While it is acknowledged that an 
aesthetically pleasing interface can evoke positive user sentiments, it constitutes merely a fraction of the overall user 
experience. The functionality of the interface gains prominence once users engage with it. Consequently, as asserted by 
experts, "User Interface (UI) serves as the initial enticement for users, while User Experience (UX) is pivotal in fostering 
a favorable impression. It is only when both elements are seamlessly integrated that customer retention can be effectively 
achieved." This study contributes valuable insights for subsequent developers based on feedback from both experts and 
users. A positive user experience plays a crucial role in shaping a favorable perception of a brand (Krug, 2000; Naylor, 
2016; Rosenfeld & Morville, 2002). Given the multitude of elements within a website that can influence user behavior, it 
is imperative to meticulously consider the impact of each detail during the development phase. This consideration becomes 
even more pertinent in light of the proliferation of cross-border e-commerce enterprises, particularly influenced by recent 
epidemics. Consequently, a comprehensive focus on the overall user experience becomes imperative not only for enhancing 
business gains but also for fostering customer loyalty retention.
This study focuses on exploring the menus of B2C retail e-commerce platforms. Given that the retail e-commerce 
industry encompasses a diverse and extensive range of products and services, it is more suitable to present the overall menu 
using mega menus to increase user operational efficiency. Consequently, this study only investigates the current trend of 
mega menus, lacking a broader exploration. However, due to the rapid development of internet technology, many different 
types of menus have been phased out without in-depth exploration. While traditional menus have disappeared in the fast-
evolving internet era, they may provide valuable insights for future menu architecture. Although this study conducts a 
literature review and defines traditional menus, it has not performed further user research on various menu types. Future 
research should focus on reviewing relevant studies and comprehensively exploring different types of menus. Additionally, 
to simplify the investigation of how menu formats are perceived and experienced psychologically by users, this study 
overlooks related website elements such as color schemes, advertising displays, and brand influences. However,
