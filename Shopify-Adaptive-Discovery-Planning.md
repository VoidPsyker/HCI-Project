# Emotional Adaptive Navigation for Shopify — Planning Document

**Research cut-off:** 21 October 2026  
**Scope:** Product, evidence, platform, legal/compliance, technical, evaluation, and business planning. This is not legal advice and contains no implementation code.

**Citation convention:** **[PAPER]** refers to one of the three supplied PDFs; **[OUTSIDE]** refers to independent legal, scientific, platform, or competitor sources; **[INFERENCE]** marks this document’s recommendation or interpretation rather than a directly demonstrated result. Legal/platform links are dated or marked with the access date in the source list.

## Executive summary

The product is **Emotional Adaptive Navigation for Shopify**: a storefront app that estimates a shopper’s likely affective state—such as frustration, confusion, uncertainty, engagement, or confidence—from non-biometric interaction signals, then adapts its own navigation and product-discovery module. The estimate must be probabilistic and reversible, never presented as a diagnosis. The supplied papers do not test emotion inference: they neither establish its effectiveness nor refute it. Independent studies provide preliminary, mixed evidence that keyboard, touch, cursor, and browsing patterns can carry affective information, but most are lab, task-specific, small, and not validated on commerce storefronts.

The defensible build is **privacy-first Emotional Adaptive Navigation**: preserve the merchant’s native menu, estimate affect only from limited non-biometric signals, adapt only the app-owned module when confidence is calibrated and high, and use an optional “how is your search going?” self-report as a direct correction signal. Do not claim accurate emotion detection or guaranteed sales lift. Avoid facial/voice recognition and persistent profiling. Shopify’s API terms, last updated 7 October 2026, restrict training AI/ML on Shopify-derived or merchant data without the relevant merchant’s consent [S8].

**Recommendation:** keep the concept and name, but sell it as an **emotion-aware, interaction-adaptive navigation aid**, not a mind reader. Start with a consented, transparent affect hypothesis model, high confidence thresholds, a static-menu fallback, and no high-pressure adaptation. Validate classification against voluntary self-reports separately from sales outcomes. Merchant demand, real-store accuracy, and incremental conversion value remain unproven. Treat EU/UK profiling and cookie rules, transfers from Karachi, and Shopify’s data-use terms as launch dependencies.

## Do this first — prioritized

1. **Keep the product promise truthful:** Emotional Adaptive Navigation estimates likely interaction affect; it does not read minds or diagnose mental health.
2. **Interview 15–20 merchants** in one catalog-heavy segment and test whether state-aware navigation solves a paid problem beyond their existing recommendations.
3. **Run a consented research-panel prototype** with non-biometric signals and an optional “how is your search going?” control; measure participant-held-out state calibration before storefront deployment.
4. **Get EU/UK, U.S., Pakistan, and Shopify/API counsel** to review the exact signal collection, consent flow, transfer map, and model-data terms.
5. **Build the MVP as an affect-aware module, not a theme takeover:** confidence-gated probabilistic rules/model, visible controls, preserved native menu, and static fallback. No camera or microphone.
6. **Test three arms:** static baseline, behavior-only personalization, and Emotional Adaptive Navigation. Analyze affect-classification accuracy separately from conversion lift.

## Emotional Adaptive Navigation: concept and signal selection

### Working definition

**Emotional Adaptive Navigation (EAN)** is a Shopify storefront feature that uses non-biometric interaction signals to estimate a shopper’s **probabilistic, transient affective state**—for example, likely frustration, confusion, uncertainty, engagement, or confidence—and adapts navigation emphasis, help prompts, layout density, and product suggestions. Each state is a hypothesis with an uncertainty/confidence score, not a diagnosis or a claim to know the shopper’s inner state. Interaction friction is a measurable pattern, not itself an emotion. The estimate is session-scoped, does not create a mental-health profile, and never changes price, eligibility, urgency, or access.

The app preserves the merchant’s native menu. It adapts only its own optional navigation/recommendation module, with a static menu fallback, visible explanation/control, and reset/disable action. “Interaction friction” is an observable signal; **frustration** is a tentative affective interpretation that requires independent validation. ### What the evidence supports—and does not

- **[PAPER]** P1 suggests a design pattern for hierarchical navigation. It does not show that adaptive menus improve outcomes, and its authors say that remains to be tested.
- **[PAPER]** P2 offers preliminary evidence that **user preference feedback** can improve perceived experience over a fixed interface in two lab domains. It does not compare preference-based adaptation with ordinary clickstream personalization and does not estimate commerce lift.
- **[PAPER]** P3 tentatively points to information architecture, menu complexity, and vertical local menus as factors affecting search performance. Its unreviewed status and method/statistical inconsistencies make the reported significance weak evidence. It says nothing about inferred emotional states.
- **[OUTSIDE]** Barrett et al.’s systematic review of facial emotion expression/perception literature (2019, 20(1), 1–68) reviewed studies across adults, children, cultures, and contexts. It found emotion-category facial patterns can occur more than chance, but are not sufficiently reliable, specific, or generalizable to diagnose a particular emotional state. It is a review, not a new shopping experiment or a single-population effect estimate. [S26]
- **[PAPER]** None of the three supplied papers measures an inferred affective state or tests emotion inference. They therefore **neither support nor refute** the EAN affect-inference component.
- **[OUTSIDE]** Peer-reviewed studies show that non-biometric signals sometimes contain affective information in constrained tasks, but results are mixed, often small, individualized, and not validated on retail navigation. Examples and limits follow.
- **[INFERENCE]** Emotional Adaptive Navigation is therefore **unproven and testable**. The evidence warrants a controlled feasibility study with self-report labels, not abandoning the concept or marketing a proven detector. Conversion uplift and state-classification validity are separate hypotheses.

Absence of a direct competitor does not prove demand or defensibility.

### Target segments and willingness to pay

Start with one niche where navigation is genuinely hard: apparel with many collections/variants, beauty with routine/concern navigation, home goods with a broad catalog, or specialty retail with technical attributes. The buyer is likely a Shopify store owner or e-commerce lead already spending on conversion, search, or merchandising. Prefer merchants able to support consented experiments and enough traffic to evaluate outcomes; low-traffic stores may still be research/design partners but cannot reliably measure incremental sales. Beauty/health and child-directed merchants need heightened legal review because affect labels and shopping context can drift into health/vulnerability inferences.

Willingness to pay is **unproven**. Do not use competitor list prices as proof. Ask merchants what problem they pay to solve today, their existing stack, the minimum measurable gain that would justify another app, and whether they would pay for a pilot. Validate on a clickable prototype/concierge before producing a Shopify extension.

### Cheap validation plan

1. **15–20 problem interviews** over two weeks; recruit one vertical with complex catalogs. Ask about frustration/uncertainty at discovery, what customers do when stuck, current app spend, and privacy/brand acceptability. Do not lead with a claim that the app can read emotion.
2. **Landing-page and prototype test:** retain the product name. Test truthful lines such as “Emotional Adaptive Navigation: adapts navigation to how shoppers interact” and “emotion-aware suggestions, with shopper control.” Measure qualified merchant demo bookings and paid-pilot commitments, not vague consumer interest.
3. **Consented concierge/research pilot:** 3–5 stores plus a separate shopper panel. Use a prototype “How’s your search going?” control; compare interaction-derived affect hypotheses to repeated voluntary self-report. Do not record raw typing or pointer paths.
4. **Go/no-go:** continue only if merchants pay/commit, the consent model is workable, and participant-held-out affect prediction performs better than a prevalence-only baseline with acceptable calibration/false positives. If sales outcomes are underpowered, say so; do not change the product name or claim success from click-through alone.

## 3. Emotional Adaptive Navigation: legal and compliance — primary launch risk

This section describes likely issues, not a legal conclusion for a particular merchant or country. The app can have merchants in the EU/UK/California while its developer is in Karachi. GDPR/UK GDPR applicability is based on the data subjects, offer/monitoring, and processing—not just where the developer lives. Ask counsel to review the final data flow and customer notice before beta.

### GDPR / UK GDPR, profiling, and lawful basis

**EU GDPR.** A store visitor’s page views, device identifiers, search queries, click paths, and inferred affect scores can be personal data when they relate to an identifiable or singled-out visitor. Systematically predicting a person’s mood, confidence, interests, or behavior from those data is likely **profiling** within Article 4(4), even if the output is only a probability and the inference is non-biometric. GDPR may apply to a Pakistan-based developer under Article 3(2) when it offers services to or monitors people in the EU. Profiling is not categorically prohibited, but requires a lawful basis, purpose limitation, minimization, transparency, security, rights handling, and potentially a DPIA. An Article 6(1)(f) legitimate-interest basis may be arguable for low-impact first-party EAN after a documented necessity/balancing test, clear notice, and objection path; it is not a blanket permission for invisible tracking, cross-site profiling, or sensitive emotion inference. Consent is often the more defensible path for optional behavioral tracking. [S1, Arts. 3–6, 13–14, 21, 25, 35]

An inferred “friction” or “uncertainty” score is **not automatically** special-category data. The risk changes if the system deliberately infers or uses mental-health status, disability, health conditions, or another Article 9 category, or if the context makes an affect score reveal health information. Keep state labels about the shopping interaction, not the person’s psychological condition; don’t infer “anxiety disorder,” “depression,” vulnerability, or capacity. [S1, Arts. 4(15), 9]

Article 22 applies to decisions based **solely** on automated processing, including profiling, that have legal or similarly significant effects. Ordinary product ranking or showing a different category is unlikely by itself to meet that threshold, but price/credit eligibility, access restrictions, sensitive categories, children, or manipulative profiling can change the analysis. Article 22 does not erase the other profiling/transparency/lawful-basis duties. Where special-category data is involved, the Article 22 exception is narrower. [S1, Arts. 9, 22; Recital 71]

**UK divergence as of this report.** The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 amended UK GDPR automated-decision provisions; the changes to Article 22 took effect **5 February 2026**. The UK now defines “solely automated” and “significant decision” in Articles 22A–22D. Significant decisions based partly on special-category data remain restricted (with specified exceptions); for other significant solely automated decisions, safeguards must provide information, representations, human intervention, and contestability. Decisions cannot rely on the new “recognised legitimate interest” basis. The ICO’s detailed post-Act guidance was updated 31 March 2026 but was still under consultation. Ordinary product recommendations are generally less likely to be significant; the ICO warns that context, children, behavior, and discriminatory or consequential offers can make a nudge significant. [S2–S3]

**Consent versus legitimate interests:** these are not interchangeable. The merchant normally determines why/how the storefront personalization works; the app may be its processor for those operations. If the developer uses customer events for its own analytics, cross-merchant benchmarking, model improvement, advertising, or another purpose, it may become an independent controller for that processing and needs its own basis/notices/rights process. Avoid that secondary use by default. For direct marketing, respect the absolute right to object to processing for direct marketing, including related profiling. Under the UK Act, the ICO says the legitimate-interests basis may be possible for some ADM, but requires a purpose/necessity/balancing assessment; UK “recognised legitimate interests” is not available for ADM. [S2–S3]

**Representatives and officers:** a non-EU developer subject to GDPR under Article 3(2) will generally need an EU representative under Article 27 unless a narrow exception applies; a similar UK representative question arises under UK GDPR. A DPO is not automatically required for a solo app, but may be mandatory if core activities involve large-scale, regular/systematic monitoring or large-scale special-category processing. Counsel should assess this from actual volume and purposes. [S1, Arts. 27, 37]

### Cookies, pixels, and ePrivacy / PECR

EU ePrivacy Directive Article 5(3) generally requires prior consent for storing information on or accessing information from a user’s terminal equipment, except for transmission and strictly necessary service functionality. This is separate from the GDPR lawful basis: legitimate interests does **not** override a cookie-consent requirement. Cookies are not the only covered mechanism; local/session storage, fingerprinting, pixels, or other access to device data can be in scope. [S4]

**UK change:** from 5 February 2026, DUAA amended PECR Regulation 6 and added Schedule A1. Alongside consent and strictly necessary exceptions, there are narrow exceptions for some statistical collection (with clear information and a free simple opt-out; it does not cover automatically emitted device data) and website appearance/functionality adaptation (clear information and simple opt-out). This might cover a user-selected UI preference in some implementations; it is **not a safe assumption** for emotion inference, granular behavioral logging, or a Shopify pixel that profiles automatically emitted events. Obtain a UK PECR review. [S5–S6]

Shopify’s current guidance says storefront `shopify:*` events fire regardless of buyer tracking consent; using them directly does not remove the developer’s consent obligations. Shopify recommends consent-aware Web Pixels for analytics/marketing, with declared purposes (`analytics`, `marketing`, `preferences`, `sale_of_data`) and its Customer Privacy API. Set only the purposes actually needed. Pixel sandboxing is not a legal exemption. [S7–S8]

### California CCPA/CPRA

The CCPA/CPRA generally covers a for-profit business doing business in California that meets a statutory threshold: annual gross revenue above **$26.625 million** (2025-adjusted), buying/selling/sharing personal information of at least 100,000 consumers/households, or deriving at least 50% of annual revenue from selling/sharing personal information. Browsing history, device identifiers, and inferences—including an affect score associated with a visitor—can be personal information. Service providers/contractors have contract-based restrictions when processing for covered businesses; using the score for the app developer’s own cross-context advertising or other unrelated purpose can change that role. The merchant will often be the business; the app may be a service provider if the written contract restricts use to specified business purposes and bars selling/sharing or unrelated use. If the app independently determines purposes, it may incur separate obligations. Support access, correction, deletion, opt-out of sale/sharing (including cross-context behavioral advertising), Global Privacy Control handling where applicable, and sensitive-personal-information restrictions as contractually assigned. Do not assume a small app is automatically outside every obligation: its merchant relationships, own business, and service-provider status matter. [S9, S31]

California’s 2025 regulations became effective **1 January 2026**, but CPPA says the ADMT significant-decision requirements begin **1 January 2027**. These apply to defined significant decisions, not automatically every product recommendation. A normal storefront recommender is unlikely to be a “significant decision”; consequential eligibility, financial, health, or similarly significant personalization needs specialist review. [S9–S10]

### Biometrics, facial analysis, and the EU AI Act

Under GDPR, biometric data is data from specific technical processing of physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics that allows or confirms unique identification; it is special-category data when processed **for uniquely identifying** a person. A photo is not automatically Article 9 biometric data merely because it depicts a face, but facial geometry templates, identification, and emotion/health inference materially increase the risk. Inferred health/mental-health data can also raise Article 9 concerns depending on what is inferred and how it is used. [S1, Arts. 4(14), 9]

Illinois BIPA is a major U.S. litigation risk: it covers “scan of face geometry” and voiceprints as biometric identifiers, with written notice/release, publicly available retention/destruction policy, and restrictions on disclosure and profit. It has a private right of action. A storefront selfie/emotion feature could implicate BIPA for Illinois residents even if the business is based elsewhere. Other state biometric/privacy laws may also apply. **Do not build this for the proposed MVP.** [S11]

The EU AI Act defines an “emotion recognition system” as inferring emotion or intention **based on biometric data**. An EAN model using only clicks, search reformulation, or other non-biometric behavior is therefore outside that specific definition on the current text; it is **not** outside GDPR profiling, ePrivacy, consumer-protection, or Shopify rules. If camera/voice/biometric inputs are added, Article 5(1)(f) prohibits emotion inference in workplaces and educational institutions (with medical/safety exceptions), and Article 50(3) requires deployers of permitted emotion-recognition/biometric-categorisation systems to inform exposed people at first exposure and process personal data under applicable privacy law. Article 5(1)(a) prohibits certain manipulative/deceptive AI techniques that materially distort behavior and cause or are likely to cause significant harm; Article 5(1)(b) addresses exploitation of age, disability, or specific social/economic vulnerability causing significant harm. This is not a ban on every product recommendation, but EAN must not exploit a state estimate to impair choice or target a vulnerable shopper. The current Commission Service Desk text is consolidated as of 27 July 2026. [S12–S13]

### Pakistan position and international transfers

**Pakistan:** the official Senate record for the 2023 private member Personal Data Protection Bill says it was introduced on 13 February 2023 and later “stands withdrawn from the Committee”; a MoITT-hosted May 2023 draft remains a draft. The official materials reviewed do not establish an enacted, comprehensive national data-protection statute as of this report. Pakistan’s Constitution Article 14 protects dignity and privacy of the home, but is not a comprehensive data-protection statute; sector/cybercrime/electronic-transaction rules still matter. Do not market “Pakistan PDPA compliance” as a substitute for review. Legislative status could have changed after the accessible record, so verify the Gazette and obtain local counsel before launch. [S14–S15, S32]

For EU visitor data accessed or stored from Karachi, Pakistan is not identified as an EU adequacy jurisdiction in the sources reviewed. If this is a restricted transfer, the EU exporter may need an appropriate Article 46 mechanism (often SCCs) and a transfer impact assessment/supplementary measures. The UK has its own transfer regime and may require an IDTA or UK Addendum. Hosting in an EU region does not necessarily eliminate a transfer if Karachi-based staff can access the data. Minimize access and have counsel map every vendor/subprocessor and support path. [S1, Arts. 44–49; S16]

### Merchant versus developer roles, notices, DPA, retention

Roles follow actual purposes, not labels in a contract. A likely starting point is: **merchant = controller/business** for storefront personalization; **app developer = processor/service provider** for operating the feature under merchant instructions. The developer is likely an independent controller for its own account administration, billing, fraud/security, and any separately decided product analytics. Joint controllership is possible if the parties jointly decide purposes/means. Have counsel map each activity.

Before beta: publish an app privacy policy (Shopify requires it), merchant terms, and an Article 28/CCPA service-provider DPA covering categories/purposes, instructions, confidentiality, security, subprocessors, international transfers, rights-request assistance, breach notice, deletion/return, audits, and no model-training/secondary use by default. Give merchants a customer-facing notice template describing the actual event tracking, personalization, consent choices, and opt-out. [S8, S18–S19]

Suggested data policy: keep interaction features in browser memory for the active session; do not persist raw clicks, text, pointer movement, or video. If a visitor opts into a test, use a random experiment assignment with a short expiry; retain only merchant/day/variant aggregates after applying a minimum cohort size. Do not include IP, raw query, customer ID, or exact timestamps in analytics. Delete tenant data after uninstall within Shopify’s 30-day terms; keep audit records only as long as needed for security/legal obligations and explain any non-deletable record. These are product recommendations, not statutory safe harbors.

### Dark-pattern and manipulation risk

The FTC’s 2022 *Bringing Dark Patterns to Light* report describes interface tactics that trick or trap consumers (e.g. hidden terms, obstructed cancellation, false urgency, and manipulative choice architecture). U.S. FTC Act §5 can reach deceptive/unfair conduct. The EU UCPD prohibits misleading/aggressive commercial practices; DSA Article 25 prohibits online platforms from designing interfaces that deceive/manipulate or materially impair free/informed decisions (its direct scope depends on the service). [S13]

The reputational line is sharper than the technical line: using a “frustration” estimate to push a higher-margin product, fake scarcity, discount, subscription, or premium checkout is a vulnerable-shopper manipulation story even if no explicit “emotion” label is visible. Keep prices, ranking logic, and major category availability stable; make suggestions visibly optional and explainable; never hide cheaper/relevant items, manufacture urgency, or use inferred stress/financial constraints to increase pressure.

## 4. Emotional Adaptive Navigation technical architecture

### Recommended Shopify fit

**Keep the scope small.** The embedded Admin app handles onboarding, merchant configuration, consent/purpose controls, experiment setup, and aggregate reports. A Theme App Extension (app block) renders the EAN navigation/recommendation module. A small app embed can collect a tightly allow-listed set of in-session interaction features; a Web Pixel is optional for separately consented evaluation events, not the adaptation engine. Use Shopify’s Storefront API `productRecommendations` as one candidate source and merchant-selected collections/tags as another. Admin GraphQL is for merchant-owned catalog/settings; avoid customer/order scopes. [S7, S17–S20, S23]

**Do not promise automatic rewriting of native menus.** Shopify’s Admin API can retrieve menu data, but Theme App Extensions do not safely replace every merchant theme’s header behavior. Theme App blocks require placement in supported theme sections; app embeds are independent blocks, not a platform-wide “reorder the header” API. In MVP, keep the merchant’s primary menu intact and show a clearly labeled alternate discovery module. If later offering a replacement navigation widget, scope it to selected compatible themes and require merchant preview/approval. [S20, S23]

### Data flow sketch

`Shopify Admin (merchant catalog + EAN settings)` → `per-shop config` → `Theme App Extension` → `in-memory session feature extractor + affect estimator` → `state probabilities + confidence` → `merchant-approved EAN module or static fallback` → `optional self-report/correction` → `consented, minimized evaluation events` → `per-store aggregate report`.

Avoid customer/order API scopes, persistent profiles, full session replay, raw keystroke/pointer capture, camera/microphone access, and checkout extensions in MVP. Keep feature extraction and estimation in the browser; server-side services handle merchant config, product candidate sync if needed, subscriptions, privacy webhooks, and consented aggregates. Do not send raw customer data, queries, or inferred state to an LLM/external model provider.

### Where inference runs

For MVP, estimate a small state vector: `P(frustration)`, `P(confusion/uncertainty)`, `P(exploratory engagement)`, and `P(goal confidence)`, plus a separate observable interaction-friction score. In early research, a rule-based score is only a provisional score; do not label it a probability or show it to shoppers until it has been calibrated against self-report on held-out participants. After feasibility passes, compare an interpretable calibrated logistic/multinomial model with the rule baseline. A score is a hypothesis, not an emotion truth. Begin with event counts and sequences, not raw trajectories: search reformulations, backtracks, repeated category changes, optional “compare” actions, coarse dwell/scroll bins, and a voluntary “How’s your search going?” response.

**Provisional confidence gates [INFERENCE; calibrate before launch]:** (a) apply only a subtle, app-module change when the top calibrated probability is ≥0.75, exceeds the second-highest state by ≥0.15, and at least two independent signal groups agree; (b) for 0.60–0.75, show only an optional factual help prompt—do not reorder; (c) below 0.60, conflicting signals, missing consent, first visit, or any model/data failure, render the static default. These thresholds are starting policy, not research-established cutoffs; set final thresholds from participant-held-out validation and a false-positive cost analysis. Explicit user selections override model scores.

Keep the affect estimate ephemeral in memory for the current session. If a shopper self-reports, use the response as an explicit current preference and a validation label only under the separately explained research consent. Never join it to a customer account or write it to a Shopify customer metafield. Browser-side inference avoids per-event network latency and reduces data sent to Karachi-hosted infrastructure.

Server-side inference is only justified if a validated use case needs catalog-wide ranking or aggregate merchant reporting. Train any general model using a separately consented research dataset, not Shopify event data. Any future per-merchant updating on Shopify-derived signals requires relevant merchant consent under the API terms, plus the shopper-side lawful basis/notice; no pooled tenant training by default. [S8]

### Stack, tenancy, and performance

**Recommendation [INFERENCE]:** Shopify’s current React Router app template with TypeScript and Polaris/App Bridge for the embedded app; Shopify Theme App Extension (Liquid + small TypeScript/JavaScript bundle) for the storefront; Admin GraphQL API for merchant setup; Storefront API for public product data; PostgreSQL for shop-level configuration and webhook state. Start without Redis, a vector database, or an ML service. Use a managed app host and managed Postgres with an EU region available; encrypt secrets/tokens with the provider’s managed key facility, use tenant/shop ID in every row and query, and add row-level isolation tests. Keep logs free of visitor IDs/raw query data. Select hosting only after reviewing subprocessors, data residency, incident terms, and a DPA.

The storefront bundle must be asynchronous/non-blocking, cache catalog/config, avoid layout shift, and never delay the merchant’s header, product rendering, cart, or checkout. Aim for single-digit KB compressed initial JS as an engineering budget, no long tasks, and client-only adaptation under a few milliseconds. If a network recommendation endpoint is slow/unavailable, render the static/default widget. Shopify’s App Store performance requirement: no more than a 10-point Lighthouse storefront score reduction; test home, product, collection on real themes. [S17, S24]

Metafields/metaobjects are suitable for merchant-owned configuration or product metadata where useful; do not store inferred visitor states in customer metafields. App-owned database settings are simpler for MVP. Admin scopes should be minimal—likely read products and any explicitly justified menu/content access; request Web Pixel scopes only if enabling the analytics feature. Shopify reviews protected data permissions individually. [S18, S25]

## 7. Emotional Adaptive Navigation evaluation and proof

### Experiments

Use two separate evaluations: **(1) affect inference validity** and **(2) whether EAN changes shopping outcomes**. Do not use a sales lift to claim the state classifier is accurate, or a classifier score to claim the feature improves commerce. Pre-register the primary outcome, state labels, event window, confidence threshold, and stopping rule.

**Commerce A/B design (three arms):**

- **A — Static control:** merchant’s fixed/native navigation and no EAN adaptation (or the existing discovery module).
- **B — Behavior-only personalization baseline:** current-session behavior changes the app module but does not infer or use an affect state (for example, a search rephrase can surface help).
- **C — Emotional Adaptive Navigation:** the same allowed signals feed the confidence-calibrated affect estimator; only high-confidence affect hypotheses modify EAN’s app-owned module. Low-confidence sessions abstain to the same static default.

Randomize at visitor/session level only where lawful and with the required consent; otherwise use a user-controlled test cohort or a carefully designed store/time switchback. Keep variant assignment stable within the permitted session, avoid overlapping merchandising experiments, stratify by device/store only using lawful aggregate data, exclude bots, and run through full weekly cycles. Predefine stopping rules; do not repeatedly peek and stop on a temporary lift. Compare **C vs B** for the incremental value of the emotion layer, and **B vs A** for the value of personalization in general.

**Affect validation panel:** recruit an opt-in panel on a representative demo/merchant storefront. At planned intervals or after a friction event, optionally ask “How’s your search going?” with choices such as “stuck,” “unsure,” “exploring,” “confident,” “doing fine,” and “skip.” A separate validated valence/arousal self-report scale can be used in a research protocol if necessary; do not ask for diagnoses or mental-health history. Treat self-report as the best available subjective criterion, not objective ground truth. Collect repeated reports per participant; record device/input method and task; include “skip.” Split training/testing **by person** and later by store/device so visits from the same person cannot leak across sets. The feasibility panel is not enough to certify broad population accuracy.

| Discovery/diagnostic | Recommendation CTR; search reformulation; time-to-first relevant product; category-to-product path completion; navigation depth; no-result search rate. These diagnose the feature; they are not equivalent to revenue. |
| UX/guardrails | One-click “helpful?”/satisfaction sample; bounce/exit; return/refund/cancellation; complaints; menu accessibility success; page load/Lighthouse/Core Web Vitals; no unintended hiding/price differences. |
| Emotion-classification validation (separate from sales) | Per-state precision/recall, macro-F1 and balanced accuracy; one-vs-rest ROC-AUC only with enough data; confusion matrix; Brier score/expected calibration error; abstention rate; false-positive rate at the chosen confidence threshold. Compare predicted affect hypotheses with the shopper’s voluntary momentary report. Never call clicks/dwell “ground truth emotion.” |
| Fairness / robustness | Repeat validation by device (touch/desktop), language/locale and accessibility/input mode where panel consent and sample sizes permit. Check class imbalance, per-group false positives, and calibration. Do not collect protected-trait data just to fill a scorecard; use accessible qualitative testing where quantitative subgroup cells are too small. |

**Power reality:** at a 2% baseline conversion, detecting an increase to 2.2% (0.2 percentage point absolute; 10% relative) with conventional 80% power and 5% two-sided significance needs roughly **77,000 eligible sessions per arm** before exclusions/cluster effects. The exact number changes with baseline, randomization, and variance. Most small Shopify stores will not reach this quickly. Report confidence intervals and minimum detectable effect, pool only with explicit merchant permission and a defensible analysis plan, and never call an underpowered test “proven.”

The emotion layer’s incremental value is **EAN (C) vs behavior-only personalization (B)**, not merely EAN vs static. The self-report panel evaluates whether behavioral signals estimate reported affect; the storefront A/B evaluates whether acting on the estimate changes outcomes. Report null results, per-state errors, calibration, and abstention. Shopify terms restrict training on Shopify-derived merchant data without consent; any use of that data for model improvement or cross-store pooling needs explicit, scoped consent and counsel review. [S8]

## 8. Emotional Adaptive Navigation: business model and go-to-market

### Pricing and costs

Test (not assume) something like **$29/month starter**, **$79/month growth**, and **$199/month multi-store/advanced reporting**, with a 14-day trial and simple cancellation. Consider a free prototype only if it does not become an unsupported support burden. Price based on features/catalog or transparent session bands; if bands require tracking, explain how sessions are counted and respect consent. These are hypotheses to test in interviews, not validated willingness-to-pay.

Shopify App Store registration is currently a **one-time $19 per Partner account**. For standard-rate developers, Shopify’s revenue share is 0% on the first $1M lifetime gross app revenue earned since 1 Jan 2025 and 15% above it, plus a **2.9% billing processing fee** and applicable taxes; eligibility differs for larger developers. New public apps should use Shopify App Pricing when supported. [S22]

**MVP operating estimate [INFERENCE]:** roughly $30–$150/month for a small managed app/database, logs, and domain/email at a few pilot stores; $100–$500/month after moderate event volume, depending on hosting and retention. Recruit through Shopify communities, agencies specializing in that vertical, and direct merchant outreach; do not imply Shopify endorsement. Use App Store categories around search/discovery and storefront design only if accurate. ## 11. Additional issues and open product decisions for Emotional Adaptive Navigation

- **Accessibility:** a moving menu can disorient keyboard, screen-reader, cognitive-accessibility, and touch users. Keep native navigation and focus order stable; obey reduced-motion; make change reversible. Accessibility testing is a requirement, not polish.
- **Incentives and conflicting goals:** “conversion” is not always customer welfare. Optimize task completion, satisfaction, and product suitability alongside sales; do not use higher AOV as the only reward.
- **Cold start:** most small stores lack enough interaction data for meaningful recommendations. Use merchant curation, product metadata, and Shopify’s existing recommendations first.
- **Shopify can close the gap:** Search & Discovery, Shop, Sidekick, theme vendors, and recommendation apps can absorb this feature. Build merchant workflow or evidence, not a vague AI moat.
- **Children and sensitive products:** avoid child-directed stores and high-risk categories (health, mental health, financial products) until counsel reviews heightened rules. Minimize retention and avoid sensitive query classification.
- **What must be decided:** one vertical; which themes are supported; what “success” means to merchants; the first release’s supported affect states; which signals need shopper consent; where data is hosted/accessed; and whether a self-report study is worth its added cost.

### Naming and marketing claims guide

Keep the product name **Emotional Adaptive Navigation**. Make the descriptor distinguish an affective estimate from a diagnosis.

For the App Store title, Shopify requires a unique, brand-led name; keep **Emotional Adaptive Navigation** intact as the product descriptor (for example, **[Distinctive Brand] — Emotional Adaptive Navigation**), and never imply Shopify endorsement.

| Can say (when the shipped behavior supports it) | Must not claim |
|---|---|
| “Emotion-aware navigation that adapts to how shoppers are interacting with your store.” | “Accurately detects each shopper’s emotion,” “reads their mind,” “knows when they are frustrated,” or clinical/mental-health diagnosis. |
| “Uses non-biometric, in-session interaction patterns to estimate when navigation help may be useful; shoppers can dismiss or reset suggestions.” | “Guaranteed conversion/AOV lift,” “reduces frustration by X%,” or fabricated/unsubstantiated performance results. Shopify App Store listing rules prohibit statistics/data claims in listing content and images, including verifiable figures. |
| “Confidence-gated suggestions; native navigation remains available.” | “Bias-free,” “works for every shopper/store,” “the first/only emotion app,” or assurances of legal compliance without substantiation. |

Use **“estimate,” “may,” “emotion-aware,”** and **“interaction patterns”** prominently. Claims on the developer website still need evidence under advertising/consumer-protection law. [S13, S17]
