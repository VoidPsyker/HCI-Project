11 Measures and instruments

The measurement plan balances three demands: capture the constructs precisely, keep repeated measurement short enough not to destroy the task, and avoid confounding the emotion manipulation with the outcome measures. The last point is easy to miss: the standard NASA-TLX includes a frustration subscale, which would overlap with the frustration induction. The plan therefore uses a workload score that excludes that subscale.

Why the NASA-TLX frustration subscale is excluded

The standard NASA-TLX has six subscales, one of which is “frustration level.” Using it after a frustration induction would make the load measure partly a manipulation check, inflating the association between state and load. The plan uses a four-item workload score (mental demand, temporal demand, effort, performance) and treats frustration as an emotion variable, not a workload variable. If a full NASA-TLX is reported for comparability, the frustration subscale is reported separately and never included in the workload composite.

11.2 Self-report timing and reactivity

Repeated self-report can interrupt navigation and make participants aware of their state. The design uses three levels of measurement:

  • Block-level: full SAM + discrete items before and after each emotion block; NASA-TLX, UMUX-Lite, disorientation, and engagement after each block. This is the primary self-report.
  • Micro-check: a 2-item valence/arousal check after trials 3 and 6 of each block (not after every trial). This provides trial-level anchors without constant interruption.
  • End-of-session: SUS per menu, preference ranking, trust/adaptation items, suspicion check, and interview. These are not repeated per trial.

For the estimator, the block-level label is the target; micro-checks are used only to assess fade. The model is trained on Study 1 block labels and features from the preceding trials. This avoids using a trial-level self-report that would be too sparse to train on.

11.4 Optional eye-tracking extension

Eye tracking is not required for the core claim, but it can explain why a menu helps. The folder’s Chiu and Yang (2024) paper provides a useful AOI/metric template. 11.5 Data-quality checks

  • Attention: one embedded instruction check (“Press 3 if you are reading this”) in the questionnaires; failure is flagged, not automatically excluded.
  • Straight-lining: long runs of identical responses; flag and inspect.
  • Completion time: questionnaire completion < 2 min or > 3 SD above the mean is flagged.
  • Technical: missing trial_end, duplicate events, clock drift, browser errors; automated validation on ingestion.
  • Randomisation: server-side audit of condition sequences; no participant sees a condition twice per state.
  • Manipulation: dominant-state rate by induction, order, and participant; report failures.
  • Bots / duplicate IDs: for online recruitment, check IP duplicates (hashed), completion patterns, and open-ended responses; exclude only with documented evidence.

12 Analysis plan

The analysis is pre-registered, model-based, and effect-size focused. The goal is not to find a significant p-value for the 6 × 4 interaction; it is to estimate the matrix with enough precision to make a defensible policy decision. Where the design is underpowered for a small effect, the paper reports the uncertainty rather than pretending the null is definitive.

12.1 Principles

  1. Pre-register the primary outcomes, the 6 × 4 interaction, the four focused contrasts, the Study 2 contrasts, and the policy rule.
  2. Analyse as randomised (intention-to-treat) for the primary test; report per-protocol and CACE as sensitivity analyses.
  3. Model the clustering of trials within participants and targets; do not treat 1,728 trials as independent.
  4. Report effect sizes with 95% CIs; exact p-values; no dichotomous “significant / not significant” language without the estimate.
  5. Correct for multiple comparisons within families; label exploratory analyses.
  6. Freeze the mapping after Study 1; do not revise it after seeing Study 2.
  7. Share the anonymised data, analysis code, task bank, and materials where ethics allow.

12.3 Primary models

Success: logit P(yijk = 1) = β0 + βmenu(j) + βstate(k) + βmenu×state(jk) + βtrial + βdifficulty + ui + vtarget
Time: log(Tijk) = α0 + αmenu(j) + αstate(k) + αmenu×state(jk) + αtrial + αdifficulty + ai + btarget + εijk

Use sum-to-zero contrasts for menu and state so that main effects are interpretable as average effects. The interaction is tested with a likelihood-ratio test (or a parametric bootstrap when the asymptotic approximation is doubtful). Estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts are computed with emmeans; p-values are adjusted within the pre-registered families. For the four focused contrasts, use Holm correction; for the exploratory 6 × 4 cell comparisons, use Benjamini–Hochberg FDR and label them exploratory.

12.4 Multiplicity, equivalence, and the “no difference” problem

A non-significant interaction is not evidence that menus are equivalent. If the interaction is small and the CI is wide, the correct conclusion is “inconclusive.” If the CI excludes a pre-specified meaningful effect, the correct conclusion is “equivalent within the margin.” The paper therefore reports:

  • The interaction estimate and its 95% CI on the log-odds / log-time scale.
  • A equivalence test for the global-best menu vs the state-specific menu, with a margin of 5 percentage points on success and 10% on time.
  • A “practical significance” statement: would the observed difference change the recommended menu under the policy rule?

12.5 Bayesian policy model

The policy model is a hierarchical Bayesian version of the primary model, fitted with brms. Regularising priors (normal(0, 1) on the log-odds scale; half-normal(0, 1) on random-effect SDs) shrink extreme cell estimates and produce posterior probabilities that each menu is best in each state. The policy rule in §8.10 is applied to the posterior predictive distribution. The Bayesian model is not used to “find significance”; it is used to quantify uncertainty and to avoid overfitting a 24-cell matrix with a modest sample.

12.6 Qualitative and process data

Retrospective think-aloud and interview data are analysed with reflexive thematic analysis. The codebook is developed inductively from a subset of transcripts, then applied to the rest; a second coder independently codes 20% of the material. Disagreements are resolved by discussion. Qualitative data are used to explain unexpected patterns, illustrate switching costs, and generate hypotheses; they are not used to replace the quantitative test. Quotes are anonymised and checked for identifying details.

12.7 Reporting and reproducibility

  • Flow diagram: participants screened, enrolled, completed, excluded, analysed.
  • Descriptive table: demographics, baseline affect, prior menu experience, colour vision, input modality.
  • Manipulation check: dominant-state rate by induction and order; mean intensity; adverse events.
  • Primary results: 6 × 4 matrix with estimates and 95% CIs; interaction test; focused contrasts; effect sizes.
  • Secondary results: usability, load, disorientation, engagement, preference; mediation.
  • Study 2/3: condition contrasts, switch cost, estimator performance, calibration, break-even curve.
  • Transparency: pre-registration link, analysis code, data dictionary, session logs, software versions, random seeds.

car::Anova(m_success, type = 3)

  data = trials_success, REML = FALSE

emmeans::emmeans(m_time, pairwise ~ menu | state, adjust = "holm")
