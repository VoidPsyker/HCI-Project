2 Research questions and hypotheses

The programme asks one overarching question and four specific ones. Each specific question maps onto a study and a pre-registered primary outcome. The hypotheses are directional where theory supports it and exploratory where it does not.

2.1 Overarching question

Which e-commerce navigation structure best supports product finding in each of four emotional contexts, and does adapting the structure to that empirically derived mapping improve performance and experience compared with a static or mismatched structure?

2.4 Primary and secondary outcomes

Primary (confirmatory)

  • Task success — correct product detail page within 120 s.
  • Completion time — log-transformed; failures censored at 120 s.
  • Navigation errors — wrong branch, wrong product, invalid transition.
  • Cognitive load — NASA-TLX after each block.
  • Perceived usability — UMUX-Lite after each block; SUS at the end.
  • Disorientation / control — 4-item scale after each block.

Secondary (exploratory / mechanism)

  • Backtracking, category switches, clicks, path efficiency.
  • Menu open/close count; hover/focus dwell; search attempts (if enabled).
  • Perceived predictability, trust, satisfaction, preference ranking.
  • Switch acceptance, reversion, perceived disruption.
  • Eye tracking (optional): time to first fixation, fixation count, scanpath, AOI transitions.
  • Individual differences: domain expertise, online shopping frequency, need for cognition.

2.5 What would count as failure — and why it is still informative

Observed patternInterpretation
No menu × context interaction; one menu wins everywhereEmotion-contingent structural adaptation is unnecessary in this domain. The applied recommendation is a stable global menu; the theoretical claim is that menu structure is robust to mild emotional context.
Interaction present, but matched adaptation does not beat staticSwitching costs, task variation, or insufficient policy precision offset the cell-level gains. This is a boundary condition, not a failed study.
Matched beats static, but mismatched also beats staticThe benefit is change/novelty/attention, not matching. The sham control and the direction of the mismatch effect distinguish these.
Matched works with known labels but not with the estimatorThe bottleneck is inference or transition policy. The break-even accuracy analysis says how much better the estimator must be.
Users frequently revert or report disorientationUser control and structural stability may outweigh performance. The design recommendation is to keep the current layout and offer adaptation as an option, not a default.

Pre-registration rule

The primary outcome, the 6 × 4 interaction test, the four state-specific focused contrasts, the Study 2 contrasts, and the policy-selection rule must be frozen before confirmatory data collection. Exploratory analyses are labelled as such. The mapping is frozen after Study 1 and must not be revised after looking at Study 2 outcomes.

3 The four emotional contexts

Emotion is treated as an experimental context, not as a clinical state and not as something that can be read directly from clicks. The four contexts are chosen because they are distinct, relevant to navigation, and measurable with brief self-report plus behavioural evidence. The system estimates self-reported context, not “true emotion.”

3.2 Why these two extra states — and why not anxiety or curiosity

The brief asked for two additional emotions beyond happiness and frustration. The recommendation is confusion/lostness and boredom/disengagement. The rationale is that they are the two states most directly implicated in navigation failure and in the engagement literature, and they occupy different quadrants of the affect space:

  • Confusion is the cognitive-affective signature of wayfinding failure. It is the state that menu structure is most obviously supposed to fix, and it is measurable through backtracking, category switching, and self-report.
  • Boredom is the low-arousal, low-engagement state that a visually rich or spatially novel menu might repair. It is the natural counterpart to confusion: one is “I don’t know where it is,” the other is “I don’t care enough to keep looking.”
  • Together with happiness (positive, moderate arousal) and frustration (negative, high arousal), they cover the valence × arousal space and add a motivational dimension that a pure circumplex misses.

3.3 Measurement model: self-report anchors, behavioural evidence, no mind-reading

Each state is measured in three ways. First, self-report is the primary label: a 9-point SAM valence and arousal rating (Bradley & Lang, 1994) plus four discrete 7-point items (happy, frustrated, confused, bored). Second, behavioural signals are logged continuously and used as noisy features for the estimator. Third, manipulation checks verify that the induction produced the intended state. The system never claims to detect emotion from behaviour alone; it predicts the self-reported context under the tested conditions.

State labels are derived with a pre-registered rule. The intended state is the label if (a) the corresponding discrete item is the highest of the four and (b) it is rated ≥ 5 on the 7-point scale. If the highest item is below 5 or two items are tied, the trial is labelled mixed/neutral and analysed separately. This avoids forcing every participant into a category and makes induction failure visible.

3.4 Induction protocol

All inductions are mild, short, and reversible. They are embedded before a block of six navigation trials, not during a trial. A neutral reset (60-s nature video or a simple filler task) separates blocks. The order of blocks is counterbalanced with a Latin square. The full scripts are in Appendix C.

Pilot criteria for a usable induction

An induction is retained only if, in the pilot (N = 8–10): (1) at least 60% of participants label the intended state as dominant; (2) mean intensity is ≥ 5/7; (3) fewer than 20% report a different state as dominant; (4) no participant reports distress above 6/10; (5) the induction takes ≤ 2.5 min including the check. If an induction fails, revise the script or replace it before the main study; do not proceed with a weak manipulation.

3.5 Carry-over, reactivity, and demand characteristics

Emotional states can carry over between blocks, and repeated self-report can make participants aware of the hypothesis. Three controls address this. First, a neutral reset and a 2-min break separate blocks. Second, the purpose of the study is described broadly as “how people find products in different website layouts,” not as “we are testing whether emotion changes menu choice.” Third, the post-experiment suspicion check asks what participants thought the study was about; participants who correctly guess the adaptation hypothesis are retained but the analysis is repeated without them as a sensitivity check.

Order effects are handled by counterbalancing the four emotion blocks across participants using a Latin square (four sequences: ABCD, BCDA, CDAB, DABC). Within each block, the six menus are presented in a second Latin square (six positions). The combination yields 24 sequences; participants are assigned randomly with stratification by gender and prior online-shopping frequency. Trial position is included as a covariate in the analysis.

What this study does not claim

It does not diagnose emotion, measure clinical anxiety or depression, or infer emotion from facial expression. It does not claim that the induced states are identical to real shopping emotions. It tests whether mild, experimentally induced, self-reported contexts moderate the relative performance of menu structures. That is a narrower and more defensible claim.
