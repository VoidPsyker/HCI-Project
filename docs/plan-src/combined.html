0 Executive summary

The project asks a simple but surprisingly untested question: if a user’s emotional context changes, should the navigation structure change with it? Most e-commerce sites keep one menu architecture for everyone. Most adaptive-menu research changes item order, highlights, or recommendations inside a fixed structure. This project compares six genuinely different structures — horizontal bar, vertical sidebar, mega menu, fractal zoom, rounded-square icon grid, and radial lattice — under four experimentally induced emotional contexts, then tests whether switching structure on the basis of that mapping actually improves product finding.

0.1 The scientific gap

Four literatures meet here but do not yet connect. Menu research shows that breadth, depth, layout, and visual format change search speed and errors (Miller, 1981; Kiger, 1984; Leuthold et al., 2011; Chiu & Yang, 2024). Adaptive-interface research shows that personalisation can improve satisfaction and engagement, but also that reordering and switching create predictability and learning costs (Findlater & McGrenere, 2004; Gajos et al., 2008; Lavie & Meyer, 2010; Gaspar-Figueiredo et al., 2025). Emotion research shows that affect shapes information processing and search persistence (Russell, 1980; Schwarz & Clore, 1983; Isen, 2001). Navigation research shows that disorientation and frustration are central failure modes (Lazar et al., 2003; Smith, 1996). What is missing is a controlled test that treats whole menu structure as the adaptive variable and emotional context as the moderator.

The existing protocol (v3.0) proposed four menus and four emotions. This plan extends it in five ways: (1) adds the rounded-square icon grid and the radial lattice, giving six structures that cover linear, panel, recursive-spatial, icon-grid, and radial geometries; (2) formalises the 6 × 4 design, randomisation, and target rotation; (3) replaces optimistic sample sizes with a simulation-based power analysis; (4) adds a causal test that separates matching from mere switching; and (5) adds an explicit estimator-error simulation so the end-to-end study can answer “how accurate must emotion inference be before adaptation pays off?”

0.2 The four emotional contexts

We keep happiness / positive affect and frustration, and add two states that are specifically diagnostic for navigation: confusion / lostness and boredom / disengagement. Together they span the two dimensions of the affect circumplex (valence and arousal) plus two states that matter uniquely for wayfinding: uncertainty about where something is (confusion) and low motivation to keep looking (boredom). Anxiety and curiosity were considered but rejected for the core design; §3.2 explains why.

What makes this a paper rather than a prototype demo

The paper does not assume “frustrated users need a sidebar.” Study 1 estimates the 6 × 4 mapping. Study 2 then freezes that mapping and tests whether using it beats (a) the best static menu and (b) a deliberately mismatched menu. Study 3 tests the complete system with a noisy estimator and a safe transition policy. A null result — for example, one menu wins everywhere, or switching costs cancel the gains — is a publishable finding because it answers the applied question “is emotion-contingent structural adaptation worth it?”

0.6 Feasibility and decisions

Full programme

N ≈ 234 across three studies; 6–9 months part-time; lab with a 1920 × 1080 desktop; optional eye tracker; ethics approval; one research assistant for counterbalancing and data checks.

Reduced programme (recommended for a semester)

Pilot + Study 1 (N = 48) + Study 2 (N = 60). Drop Study 3 or run it as a small feasibility study (N = 24) without a deployable estimator. This still yields a complete paper: mapping + causal test + a simulation of estimator error.

Minimum publishable unit

Pilot + Study 1 with N = 48 and a focused test of four pre-registered state-specific contrasts. This is enough for a CHI Extended Abstract or a thesis chapter, but the causal claim is weaker.

What the plan deliberately does not do

  • It does not use webcam, facial expression, or biometric “emotion detection.” Self-report anchors the state; interaction signals are treated as noisy evidence.
  • It does not assume that one menu is “best” before the data. Directional predictions are stated, but the mapping is estimated.
  • It does not change the menu mid-route. Switches happen at task boundaries, with an explanation and a keep-current option in Study 3.
  • It does not hide null results. A global winner, a failed induction, or a switching-cost null are all informative.

1 Background and related work

This project sits at the intersection of menu design, adaptive user interfaces, and affective computing. The literature is rich in each area, but the three are rarely combined in a single controlled experiment. This section summarises what is established, what the four supplied papers contribute, and where the present study adds new evidence.

1.1 Menu structure is not a neutral container

Decades of menu research show that the shape of a hierarchy changes search behaviour. The classic breadth–depth trade-off finds that broader, shallower menus are faster and less error-prone than deep, narrow ones, at least within the ranges tested (Miller, 1981; Kiger, 1984; Snowberry et al., 1983). Two-level menus often outperform deeper hierarchies (Parkinson et al., 1988), which is one reason the mega menu — a panel that exposes several levels at once — can be efficient (Li & Nielsen, 2017). But breadth is not free: a panel with many links increases visual density and the number of distractors, and it can make category boundaries harder to parse.

Orientation matters too. Leuthold et al. (2011) used eye tracking to compare vertical, dynamic, and simple menus and found that vertical menus required less visual effort and produced faster, more successful navigation. Chiu and Yang (2024) extended this to e-commerce menu formats, comparing a horizontal main menu with a vertical local menu and a fully horizontal version. Their eye-tracking data showed that a horizontal main menu plus a vertical local menu improved visual efficiency and satisfaction relative to a dense, multi-category horizontal layout. The practical lesson is not “vertical is always better”; it is that the fit between orientation, density, and task matters, and that subjective and physiological measures can diverge.

Visual form also matters. Ahlström et al. (2010) showed that square menus can be faster than traditional hierarchical menus because they exploit the full 2D space and reduce pointing time. Radial menus exploit direction and distance in a different way, and Fitts’s law predicts that targets arranged around a central hub can be acquired quickly when the direction is known (Fitts, 1954). The rounded-square icon grid and the radial lattice in this study are not arbitrary novelties; they manipulate spatial geometry, visual salience, and motor trajectory, which are exactly the dimensions that menu research says should matter.

1.2 Adaptive menus: benefits, costs, and the switching problem

Adaptive menus change item order, prominence, or availability based on usage. Findlater and McGrenere (2004) compared static, adaptive, and adaptable menus and found that adaptation can help, but user control and awareness moderate the benefit. Gajos et al. (2008) showed that predictability and accuracy trade off: an adaptive interface that moves items can be faster in the moment but slower overall if users cannot predict where things are. Lavie and Meyer (2010) catalogue the benefits and costs of adaptive user interfaces, including the cost of re-learning and the risk of maladaptive changes. This is the core tension for the present project: a structure that fits the current state may be better, but changing structure destroys spatial memory and imposes a transition cost.

The supplied RL paper (Gaspar-Figueiredo et al., 2025) is a useful counterpoint. It extends an RL-based adaptive-UI framework with individual human feedback: each participant ranks pairs of adaptation videos, a personalised reward modifier is trained, and a unique RL agent is trained for that participant. In a crossover study with 33 participants across an e-learning and a trip-planning domain, the adaptive interface with human feedback produced higher user satisfaction (estimated −0.97 for non-adaptive, p < .001) and higher engagement (−0.23, p = .016) than the non-adaptive interface. The paper is important because it shows that individualised feedback can make adaptation work, and because it carefully separates adaptive from non-adaptive baselines. But it adapts layout, font size, density, theme, and widget type — not whole menu architectures — and it measures UX rather than product-finding performance. It also does not test whether the adaptation remains beneficial when the preference model is wrong, nor does it quantify switching costs.

The fractal menu paper (Sahraoui et al., 2025) contributes a different idea: use the entire display as a recursive, self-similar hierarchy. Each menu level is a rectangular area; selecting an area zooms into its children; selecting outside the area zooms out; a breadcrumb supports direct return. The authors engineer the menu with an L-system, validate it in a steel-mill checklist use case, and argue that self-similarity supports proprioceptive memory and orientation in deep hierarchies. Crucially, the paper is a concept and engineering paper with a use-case validation, not a controlled comparison. It explicitly says that empirical demonstration remains future work. The present study provides that empirical test, and adds the emotional moderator the fractal paper does not consider.

1.3 Emotion, affect, and information seeking

Emotion is not a single variable. The most widely used descriptive model is the valence–arousal circumplex: affective states vary in how pleasant they are and how activated they are (Russell, 1980). Mehrabian and Russell’s (1974) PAD model adds dominance, which is relevant to navigation because perceived control is a core part of the experience. Appraisal theories add cognitive structure: emotions arise from evaluations of goal relevance, goal congruence, agency, and certainty (Ortony et al., 1988). This matters because frustration, confusion, and boredom are not just points on a circle; they have different appraisals and different action tendencies.

Affect influences how people process information. Positive affect tends to broaden attention and increase exploratory behaviour (Isen, 2001), which may make a visually richer or more exploratory menu more acceptable. Negative affect narrows attention and increases vigilance, which may make a dense panel feel overwhelming. The affect-as-information account (Schwarz & Clore, 1983) predicts that people may use their current feelings as a cue when judging an interface, so the same menu can be evaluated differently depending on mood. In navigation specifically, disorientation is a well-documented source of frustration (Lazar et al., 2003), and lostness has been operationalised as the mismatch between the user’s expected path and the actual path (Smith, 1996). Boredom is the mirror image: low arousal, low interest, and reduced exploration, which can lead to fast guessing or abandonment (O’Brien et al., 2018).

Consumer research adds a domain-specific taxonomy. Richins’ (1997) Consumption Emotions Set and Laros and Steenkamp’s (2005) hierarchical model distinguish positive and negative consumption emotions, including contentment, excitement, frustration, and disappointment. The present study uses a smaller, experimentally tractable set because repeated measurement of a large taxonomy is impractical. The four states are selected for their theoretical distinctness, relevance to navigation, and measurability with brief self-report plus interaction signals.

Why the mapping must be empirical

These predictions conflict in interesting ways. For example, under frustration, C is predicted to help because it reduces clicks, but it also presents 18 links at once, which may increase visual load. Under confusion, B is predicted to help because it is persistent, but a persistent tree can also be overwhelming. The study is designed to resolve these trade-offs, not to confirm a slogan.

1.6 Contributions

  1. Empirical: the first 6 × 4 matrix of menu-structure performance and experience across experimentally induced emotional contexts, with uncertainty estimates.
  2. Causal: a test of whether emotion-matched structural switching beats a static best menu and a mismatched menu, separating matching from mere change.
  3. Methodological: a simulation-based power analysis for the 6 × 4 design, a menu-parity checklist, and a break-even accuracy analysis for emotion inference.
  4. Applied: a reusable open-source prototype and event schema for comparing heterogeneous menu architectures over one taxonomy.
  5. Theoretical: evidence on whether the benefit of adaptation is explained by cognitive load, spatial memory, perceived control, or visual engagement.

3 The four emotional contexts

Emotion is treated as an experimental context, not as a clinical state and not as something that can be read directly from clicks. The four contexts are chosen because they are distinct, relevant to navigation, and measurable with brief self-report plus behavioural evidence. The system estimates self-reported context, not “true emotion.”

3.2 Why these two extra states — and why not anxiety or curiosity

The brief asked for two additional emotions beyond happiness and frustration. The recommendation is confusion/lostness and boredom/disengagement. The rationale is that they are the two states most directly implicated in navigation failure and in the engagement literature, and they occupy different quadrants of the affect space:

  • Confusion is the cognitive-affective signature of wayfinding failure. It is the state that menu structure is most obviously supposed to fix, and it is measurable through backtracking, category switching, and self-report.
  • Boredom is the low-arousal, low-engagement state that a visually rich or spatially novel menu might repair. It is the natural counterpart to confusion: one is “I don’t know where it is,” the other is “I don’t care enough to keep looking.”
  • Together with happiness (positive, moderate arousal) and frustration (negative, high arousal), they cover the valence × arousal space and add a motivational dimension that a pure circumplex misses.

3.3 Measurement model: self-report anchors, behavioural evidence, no mind-reading

Each state is measured in three ways. First, self-report is the primary label: a 9-point SAM valence and arousal rating (Bradley & Lang, 1994) plus four discrete 7-point items (happy, frustrated, confused, bored). Second, behavioural signals are logged continuously and used as noisy features for the estimator. Third, manipulation checks verify that the induction produced the intended state. The system never claims to detect emotion from behaviour alone; it predicts the self-reported context under the tested conditions.

State labels are derived with a pre-registered rule. The intended state is the label if (a) the corresponding discrete item is the highest of the four and (b) it is rated ≥ 5 on the 7-point scale. If the highest item is below 5 or two items are tied, the trial is labelled mixed/neutral and analysed separately. This avoids forcing every participant into a category and makes induction failure visible.

3.4 Induction protocol

All inductions are mild, short, and reversible. They are embedded before a block of six navigation trials, not during a trial. A neutral reset (60-s nature video or a simple filler task) separates blocks. The order of blocks is counterbalanced with a Latin square. The full scripts are in Appendix C.

Pilot criteria for a usable induction

An induction is retained only if, in the pilot (N = 8–10): (1) at least 60% of participants label the intended state as dominant; (2) mean intensity is ≥ 5/7; (3) fewer than 20% report a different state as dominant; (4) no participant reports distress above 6/10; (5) the induction takes ≤ 2.5 min including the check. If an induction fails, revise the script or replace it before the main study; do not proceed with a weak manipulation.

3.5 Carry-over, reactivity, and demand characteristics

Emotional states can carry over between blocks, and repeated self-report can make participants aware of the hypothesis. Three controls address this. First, a neutral reset and a 2-min break separate blocks. Second, the purpose of the study is described broadly as “how people find products in different website layouts,” not as “we are testing whether emotion changes menu choice.” Third, the post-experiment suspicion check asks what participants thought the study was about; participants who correctly guess the adaptation hypothesis are retained but the analysis is repeated without them as a sensitivity check.

Order effects are handled by counterbalancing the four emotion blocks across participants using a Latin square (four sequences: ABCD, BCDA, CDAB, DABC). Within each block, the six menus are presented in a second Latin square (six positions). The combination yields 24 sequences; participants are assigned randomly with stratification by gender and prior online-shopping frequency. Trial position is included as a covariate in the analysis.

What this study does not claim

It does not diagnose emotion, measure clinical anxiety or depression, or infer emotion from facial expression. It does not claim that the induced states are identical to real shopping emotions. It tests whether mild, experimentally induced, self-reported contexts moderate the relative performance of menu structures. That is a narrower and more defensible claim.

The click-depth problem — state it, do not hide it

The mega menu (C) reaches a leaf in two clicks; the other five need three. That is a real advantage of mega menus and a plausible reason frustrated users like them. But it means a C-vs-A difference cannot be attributed to “layout” alone. The analysis therefore reports (a) the raw structural comparison, (b) a mediation analysis with clicks as the mediator, and (c) a matched-depth sensitivity analysis on A/B/D/E/F only. If the C advantage disappears when clicks are equated, the paper says so. This is more honest than adding a fake third click to C and pretending the menus are equal.

5.2 Task design

The primary task is known-item product finding: the participant is given a short, natural-language shopping goal and must reach the correct product detail page using the menu. Difficulty is manipulated as a within-participant covariate, not as a fully crossed factor. Three levels are defined by three orthogonal properties: (1) serial position of the department and subcategory in the menu, (2) semantic distance between the task wording and the leaf label, and (3) number of plausible distractor leaves with the same colour or material. Each participant sees 24 targets, balanced 8 easy / 8 medium / 8 hard. The assignment of difficulty to the 24 menu × state cells is rotated across participants so difficulty cannot be confounded with a condition.

The programme is sequential by design. Study 1 estimates the mapping; Study 2 freezes it and tests causality; Study 3 tests the complete system with a noisy estimator. This avoids the circularity of deriving a policy and testing it on the same data.

6.3 Randomisation and counterbalancing

Study 1 has two crossed order factors: four emotion blocks and six menus within each block. A full randomisation of 24 cells would confound order with the cell-specific targets at small N. Instead, use two Latin squares:

  • Emotion block order: four sequences — ABCD, BCDA, CDAB, DABC — assigned evenly across participants. Each state appears in each serial position exactly once across the four sequences.
  • Menu order within block: a 6 × 6 balanced Latin square, so each menu appears in each within-block position once. A different row is used for each block to avoid the same menu always following the same state.
  • Target rotation: six target sets of 24 tasks, each balanced by department and difficulty. Across participants, each target set is paired with each block order and menu order equally often. The full matrix is in Appendix E.
  • Practice: one practice trial per menu (not analysed) before the main blocks, to separate menu learning from the experimental effect. Practice targets are not reused in the main trials.
  • Breaks: a 2-min neutral reset between blocks; participants can take a longer break without losing compensation.

Study 2 uses the same block-order logic but four conditions per state. The four conditions are presented in a 4 × 4 Latin square within each block; across participants, each condition appears in each position equally often. Study 3 has two conditions (static, hybrid) and uses an ABBA/BAAB crossover to balance order and carry-over.

6.5 Recruitment, inclusion, and compensation

Recruit adults aged 18–65 who are fluent in English and have normal or corrected-to-normal vision. No prior participation in the pilot or in another study of the programme. Colour vision is screened with a short Ishihara-style test; the palette is designed to be distinguishable for the most common deficiencies, so colour-vision deficiency is not an automatic exclusion — it is recorded and analysed as a covariate. Participants must be able to use a mouse or trackpad; keyboard-only users can be accommodated in a separate session.

Use a university participant pool and a reputable online panel (e.g., Prolific) for the reduced design. Compensate at £12–£15 per hour or course credit with a comparable alternative. Do not use performance-contingent bonuses: they would confound emotion induction and risk pressure. Target a 20% attrition/technical-failure buffer; replace participants who fail the pre-registered data-quality checks, not those whose results are inconvenient.

Stopping and data-quality rules

8.6 Trial procedure (exact sequence)

  1. Task screen (5 s or until “Start”): the shopping goal is displayed in neutral language. The participant clicks “Start” when ready.
  2. Navigation: the menu appears with the store home page. The participant uses the menu to reach the product. Search is visibly disabled. The 120-s timer starts at “Start.”

  4. Timeout / give-up: at 120 s, or if the participant presses “I can’t find it,” the trial ends with “Time is up. Moving on.” No performance feedback, praise, or criticism.
  5. Inter-trial interval: 3 s blank screen with a fixation cross, then the next task.
  6. Check-in: after trials 3 and 6 of each block, a 2-item micro-check (valence and arousal) appears; the full discrete-emotion check appears at the end of the block. This limits reactivity while providing trial-level anchors.

Why neutral feedback? Praise or error messages would induce additional emotion and contaminate the block. The product page itself is the success signal. The experimenter does not comment on performance during the session.

8.9 Analysis plan

The primary analysis is a set of mixed-effects models with menu, state, and their interaction as fixed effects, plus trial position, difficulty, and target set as covariates. Participant and target are crossed random intercepts. A random slope for menu by participant is included if the model converges and the random-effect structure is supported by a likelihood-ratio test; otherwise the simpler model is reported with a sensitivity analysis.

Success: logit P(yijk = 1) = β0 + βmenu(j) + βstate(k) + βmenu×state(jk) + βtrial + βdifficulty + ui + vtarget
Time: log(Tijk) = α0 + αmenu(j) + αstate(k) + αmenu×state(jk) + αtrial + αdifficulty + ai + btarget + εijk
  • Success is analysed with a binomial GLMM (glmer, logit link). Report odds ratios with 95% CIs. The interaction is tested with a likelihood-ratio test against a model without the interaction; a parametric bootstrap provides a second p-value if the asymptotic test is questionable.
  • Time is analysed with a linear mixed model on the log scale. Failures are right-censored at 120 s; a Cox frailty model (participant random effect) is the pre-registered sensitivity analysis. Report geometric means and percentage differences.
  • Errors are counts; use a negative-binomial GLMM with an offset for opportunities. Report incidence-rate ratios.
  • Load, usability, disorientation are analysed with LMMs (or ordinal models for individual items). Report estimated marginal means with 95% CIs.
  • Multiplicity: the omnibus interaction is the gate. If it is not significant, the four focused contrasts are reported as exploratory with FDR-adjusted p-values. If it is significant, the four contrasts are tested with Holm correction and reported alongside the full matrix.
  • Effect sizes: odds ratios, percentage-point differences in success, Cohen’s dz for within-subject time contrasts, and incidence-rate ratios. Report precision, not just p-values.
  • Model checks: residual plots, overdispersion, singularity, influence diagnostics; report if a simpler random-effects structure was used.
  • Sensitivity: exclude C (click-depth confound); analyse A/B/D/E/F only; per-protocol state labels; exclude participants who guessed the hypothesis; exclude the first trial of each block (learning); include input modality as a covariate.

8.10 From matrix to policy

The output of Study 1 is not a single “best menu.” It is a 6 × 4 matrix with uncertainty. The policy is selected with a pre-registered, transparent rule:

  1. Guardrail: for each state, retain menus whose success rate is within 5 percentage points of the best menu in that state (non-inferiority margin). If no menu is clearly best, use the global best menu for that state.
  2. Efficiency: among retained menus, choose the one with the shortest geometric mean completion time.
  3. Errors: if two menus are within 10% on time, choose the one with fewer navigation errors.
  4. Experience: if still tied, choose the higher UMUX-Lite / lower NASA-TLX.
  5. Uncertainty: require a posterior probability ≥ 0.90 that the chosen menu is best on the primary outcome, or a clear non-inferiority result. Otherwise default to the global best menu and flag the state as “no reliable adaptation.”
  6. Simplicity: if the chosen menu is within the guardrail of the global best, prefer the global best to avoid switching costs.

A Bayesian hierarchical model (brms) with regularising priors on the 24 cell effects provides posterior probabilities and shrinks extreme cell estimates. The frequentist models remain the primary inferential analysis; the Bayesian model is used for policy selection and for communicating uncertainty. A utility-based sensitivity analysis (weights on success, time, errors, usability) checks whether the policy changes under reasonable alternative weights.

9 Study 2 — causal test of adaptation

Study 1 can show that menus differ across states. It cannot show that using that mapping helps. Study 2 freezes the mapping and tests the causal claim against three counterfactuals: the best static menu, a deliberately mismatched menu, and a sham switch that changes nothing but announces a change.

9.1 Objective and logic

The core comparison is matched vs static. If the matched condition is better, the mapping has applied value. But a gain could come from mere change, novelty, or attention to the interface rather than from matching. The mismatched condition controls for change without fit; the sham condition controls for the announcement of change without any actual change. The four conditions form a 2 × 2 logic:

9.7 Analysis

The primary model is a mixed-effects model with condition, state, and condition × state as fixed effects, plus period, sequence, and target set as covariates. Participant and target are crossed random intercepts. Planned contrasts are matched vs static, matched vs mismatched, matched vs sham, and static vs mismatched. Report the full condition × state matrix even if the interaction is not significant.

  • Carry-over: include period and sequence; test condition × period; if carry-over is present, analyse only the first period or use a washout model.
  • Concordance: exclude concordant states from the matched-vs-static contrast; report the number of discordant states and the per-state results.
  • Per-protocol: if a participant clicks “dislike” or does not attend to the transition, the trial remains in the ITT analysis; a per-protocol analysis excludes trials with no detectable menu inspection.
  • Mediation: test whether the matched effect is mediated by switching cost, perceived disruption, cognitive load, and disorientation. Use bootstrapped indirect effects with 5,000 resamples.
  • Equivalence: if matched and static are statistically indistinguishable, report an equivalence test against a pre-specified margin (5 pp success; 10% time) rather than claiming “no difference.”
  • Bayesian: a hierarchical model with regularising priors provides the posterior probability that matched > static and that matched > mismatched; use it for interpretation, not for p-hacking.

10 Study 3 — end-to-end system with an imperfect estimator

Study 2 uses known, induced states. A real system must infer the state from noisy signals. Study 3 tests whether the adaptation still helps when the state estimate is uncertain, and it identifies the accuracy at which adaptation stops paying for itself.

10.1 Objective and design logic

The study compares a static condition (the global best menu) with a hybrid adaptive condition that uses a frozen estimator, a confidence threshold, and a safe transition policy. The estimator is trained on Study 1 and validated with participant-grouped cross-validation; it is not trained on Study 3 outcomes. The design is a within-subject crossover: four emotion blocks, each with one static trial and one hybrid trial in counterbalanced order, plus a short free-browsing task. This yields paired comparisons within each state while keeping the session under 75 minutes.

The estimator predicts the participant’s self-reported dominant state at the start of a task, using (a) the block-level self-report check-in and (b) interaction features from the previous task. Compare with a majority-class baseline and with an “oracle” model that knows the induced state. The gap between the estimator and the oracle is the inference cost.

10.3 Transition policy

A prediction is not an instruction. The system changes the menu only when five conditions hold:

  1. Confidence: the calibrated probability of the predicted state is ≥ 0.70 (tuned on Study 1, frozen before Study 3).
  2. Expected gain: the predicted utility gain of the target menu over the current menu exceeds a pre-registered minimal meaningful gain (e.g., 5% of the current expected utility).
  3. Dwell: the current menu has been used for at least two trials, so the system does not oscillate.
  4. Boundary: the switch happens between tasks, not mid-route.
  5. User control: the banner offers “Keep current” and “Switch back”; a revert is logged and respected for the rest of the block.

If confidence is below threshold or the model sees an out-of-distribution feature pattern, the system keeps the current menu and logs “no adaptation.” This fallback is a feature, not a failure: a system that abstains when uncertain is safer than one that always switches.

N = 72 (full) or N = 24–48 (feasibility). The full sample gives ~80% power for a paired difference of d = 0.35 on the hybrid-vs-static condition mean and a 95% CI half-width of about ±0.06 for a macro-F1 near .55 (assuming four check-ins per participant). The reduced sample is a feasibility study, not a confirmatory estimator evaluation; report precision and do not overclaim. Inclusion/exclusion are the same as Study 1, with no overlap.

10.7 Analysis

  • Estimator: participant-grouped cross-validation; report the mean and 95% CI across folds; compare with the majority baseline and the oracle. A model that cannot beat the baseline by a meaningful margin is reported as a failure, not hidden.
  • System effect: paired mixed-effects models with condition, state, and condition × state; participant and target random intercepts; period and sequence covariates. Primary contrast: hybrid vs static on success (non-inferiority) and time/errors (superiority).
  • Confidence stratification: split hybrid trials into high-confidence and low-confidence predictions; test whether the benefit is concentrated in high-confidence trials. This is the key mechanism test.
  • Switch cost: compare the first trial after an accepted switch with stable trials; estimate the cost and the number of trials needed to recover.
  • Break-even simulation: use the Study 1 6 × 4 matrix as the ground truth. Generate synthetic predictions with confusion matrices at 40%, 55%, 70%, 85%, and 100% accuracy; apply the same confidence/hysteresis policy; compute expected success, time, and utility. Plot utility against accuracy and identify the break-even point where hybrid = static. This can be done without new participants and is a standalone contribution.
  • Decision curve: net benefit of adaptation across confidence thresholds; choose the threshold that maximises net benefit on calibration data, then freeze it for Study 3.
  • Qualitative: interview data on trust, notice, and perceived intelligence; thematic analysis; use quotes to illustrate, not to replace the quantitative test.

Why the NASA-TLX frustration subscale is excluded

The standard NASA-TLX has six subscales, one of which is “frustration level.” Using it after a frustration induction would make the load measure partly a manipulation check, inflating the association between state and load. The plan uses a four-item workload score (mental demand, temporal demand, effort, performance) and treats frustration as an emotion variable, not a workload variable. If a full NASA-TLX is reported for comparability, the frustration subscale is reported separately and never included in the workload composite.

11.2 Self-report timing and reactivity

Repeated self-report can interrupt navigation and make participants aware of their state. The design uses three levels of measurement:

  • Block-level: full SAM + discrete items before and after each emotion block; NASA-TLX, UMUX-Lite, disorientation, and engagement after each block. This is the primary self-report.
  • Micro-check: a 2-item valence/arousal check after trials 3 and 6 of each block (not after every trial). This provides trial-level anchors without constant interruption.
  • End-of-session: SUS per menu, preference ranking, trust/adaptation items, suspicion check, and interview. These are not repeated per trial.

For the estimator, the block-level label is the target; micro-checks are used only to assess fade. The model is trained on Study 1 block labels and features from the preceding trials. This avoids using a trial-level self-report that would be too sparse to train on.

12 Analysis plan

The analysis is pre-registered, model-based, and effect-size focused. The goal is not to find a significant p-value for the 6 × 4 interaction; it is to estimate the matrix with enough precision to make a defensible policy decision. Where the design is underpowered for a small effect, the paper reports the uncertainty rather than pretending the null is definitive.

12.1 Principles

  1. Pre-register the primary outcomes, the 6 × 4 interaction, the four focused contrasts, the Study 2 contrasts, and the policy rule.
  2. Analyse as randomised (intention-to-treat) for the primary test; report per-protocol and CACE as sensitivity analyses.
  3. Model the clustering of trials within participants and targets; do not treat 1,728 trials as independent.
  4. Report effect sizes with 95% CIs; exact p-values; no dichotomous “significant / not significant” language without the estimate.
  5. Correct for multiple comparisons within families; label exploratory analyses.
  6. Freeze the mapping after Study 1; do not revise it after seeing Study 2.
  7. Share the anonymised data, analysis code, task bank, and materials where ethics allow.

12.4 Multiplicity, equivalence, and the “no difference” problem

A non-significant interaction is not evidence that menus are equivalent. If the interaction is small and the CI is wide, the correct conclusion is “inconclusive.” If the CI excludes a pre-specified meaningful effect, the correct conclusion is “equivalent within the margin.” The paper therefore reports:

  • The interaction estimate and its 95% CI on the log-odds / log-time scale.
  • A equivalence test for the global-best menu vs the state-specific menu, with a margin of 5 percentage points on success and 10% on time.
  • A “practical significance” statement: would the observed difference change the recommended menu under the policy rule?

12.5 Bayesian policy model

The policy model is a hierarchical Bayesian version of the primary model, fitted with brms. Regularising priors (normal(0, 1) on the log-odds scale; half-normal(0, 1) on random-effect SDs) shrink extreme cell estimates and produce posterior probabilities that each menu is best in each state. The policy rule in §8.10 is applied to the posterior predictive distribution. The Bayesian model is not used to “find significance”; it is used to quantify uncertainty and to avoid overfitting a 24-cell matrix with a modest sample.

12.6 Qualitative and process data

Retrospective think-aloud and interview data are analysed with reflexive thematic analysis. The codebook is developed inductively from a subset of transcripts, then applied to the rest; a second coder independently codes 20% of the material. Disagreements are resolved by discussion. Qualitative data are used to explain unexpected patterns, illustrate switching costs, and generate hypotheses; they are not used to replace the quantitative test. Quotes are anonymised and checked for identifying details.

The sample sizes in this plan come from a Monte Carlo simulation of the actual design, not from a rule of thumb. The plan is powered for a medium interaction, and it uses four focused contrasts to recover power for the specific predictions.

13.8 If you cannot reach the target N

  • Reduce the state factor: run a 6 × 2 design (happy vs frustrated) with N = 48; this halves the trials and increases power per cell. Treat the other two states as a separate exploratory study.
  • Use focused contrasts: pre-register two or three state-specific comparisons rather than the omnibus; report the omnibus as descriptive.
  • Add trials per cell: two targets per cell (48 trials) or a second session; this increases precision but raises fatigue and cost. Model the additional targets as a random effect.
  • Multi-site: run the same protocol at two labs with a shared pre-registration and a site random effect.
  • Be honest: report the achieved precision and label the study as underpowered for small effects. A well-reported inconclusive result is more valuable than an overclaimed significant one.

The one-sentence sample-size story

For a semester project, N = 48 in Study 1 and N = 60 in Study 2 is the practical minimum for a credible paper, powered for medium effects; for a full paper, use N = 72 and N = 80 and add Study 3. Do not run the full 6 × 4 with N = 24 and interpret a null interaction as evidence that emotion does not matter.

14.3 Randomisation service

Randomisation is server-side and auditable. The service pre-generates balanced sequences from a seeded PRNG and assigns each participant to the next sequence in a stratified block. The client never chooses a condition. The audit table stores the sequence ID, block order, menu orders, target set, and a hash of the seed.

// Pseudocode — server-side assignment
sequences = generateBalancedDesign({
  blockOrders: latinSquare(4),      // ABCD, BCDA, CDAB, DABC

onJoin(participant):

  seq = nextUnused(sequences, stratum)
  audit(participant.id, seq.id, hash(CONFIG.seed))

Balance checks run nightly: each block order, menu position, and target set should be used with approximately equal frequency. If a stratum is exhausted, generate a new block rather than reusing sequences. Any deviation is logged and reported in the paper’s supplementary materials.

onTaskBoundary(participant, currentMenu):
  features = extractFeatures(participant.recentTrials)
  prior = participant.blockCheckin          // 1-item state prior
  posterior = estimator.predict(features, prior)
  state = argmax(posterior)
  confidence = max(posterior)

  if confidence < 0.70:
      log("no_adaptation", reason="low_confidence")
      return currentMenu

  target = policy[state]
  gain = expectedUtility(target, state) - expectedUtility(currentMenu, state)

  if gain < MIN_GAIN or dwell < 2 or recentSwitch:
      log("no_adaptation", reason="guardrail")
      return currentMenu

  showBanner(target, reason="support_current_task")
  if user.keepsCurrent:
      log("adaptation_rejected")
      return currentMenu

  log("adaptation_accepted", state, confidence, gain)
  return target

The engine logs every decision, including abstentions. This is essential for the analysis: a system that adapts rarely but well is different from one that adapts often and badly, even if their average task performance is similar.

15 Ethics, privacy, and risk

The study induces mild emotions and observes navigation behaviour. Neither is inherently harmful, but both require care: the frustration induction can feel manipulative, the confusion task can make participants feel incompetent, and the interaction logs can become identifying if handled casually. The ethics plan is therefore part of the design, not a formality at the end.

15.1 Approval and oversight

Obtain approval from the relevant institutional research ethics committee before recruitment or pilot data collection. The protocol, consent forms, induction scripts, questionnaires, data-management plan, and debrief must be approved. Any change to the induction, the deception, the data collected, or the sample requires an amendment. If the study is conducted across institutions, obtain approval at each site and a data-sharing agreement.

15.2 Informed consent

Participants receive a plain-language information sheet covering: the purpose (comparing website layouts for product finding); what they will do; the duration; the mild mood tasks; the fact that the interface may change; the data collected (clicks, timing, questionnaire responses, optionally gaze); how data are stored and shared; the right to withdraw without penalty; compensation; and contact details. Consent is recorded electronically or on paper, separate from the research data.

Consent must mention the mood tasks without revealing the hypothesis

“This study compares different website navigation layouts. You will complete short product-finding tasks and brief mood exercises, including a short comedy clip, a search task that may not work as expected, an ambiguous categorisation task, and a repetitive catalogue task. These are mild and temporary. You can stop at any time.” This is honest about what happens without telling participants that we are testing whether frustration changes menu choice.

The study uses mild deception: the “buggy search” is intentional, and the purpose is described broadly rather than as an emotion-and-menu study. If the system were deployed commercially, it would need a separate fairness, transparency, and consent review.

16 Validity, limitations, and falsification

A strong paper is not one that hides its weaknesses; it is one that anticipates them and designs around them. This section states the threats to inference and the limits of what the study can claim. It also says what evidence would falsify the core hypothesis.

16.2 Construct validity

  • Emotion labels: self-report measures the participant’s interpreted state, not a latent “true” emotion. The paper uses “self-reported emotional context” and avoids clinical language.
  • Induction: a buggy search is a plausible frustration trigger, but it is not the same as real purchase frustration. The ambiguity task is a proxy for category confusion, not for all confusion.
  • Menu constructs: “structure” is a bundle of geometry, breadth, depth, and persistence. The paper reports the bundle and uses mediation/sensitivity to decompose it; it does not claim to isolate a single dimension.
  • Outcomes: success and time are objective but task-specific; usability and load are self-reported; engagement is inferred from self-report and behaviour. No single measure is treated as the whole construct.
  • Questionnaires: SUS, UMUX-Lite, NASA-TLX, and SAM are established, but they were not designed for this exact context; pilot reliability and comprehension checks are required.

16.3 External validity

DimensionThis studyLimit / future work

16.4 Statistical conclusion validity

  • Power: the 6 × 4 omnibus is powered for medium effects; small effects will be inconclusive. The paper reports CIs and does not interpret a non-significant interaction as proof of no effect.
  • Multiplicity: pre-registered families; Holm for focused contrasts; FDR for exploratory cells; all analyses labelled.
  • Model assumptions: random-effects structure, linearity on the log scale, distributional assumptions; diagnostics and sensitivity models.
  • Missing data: failures are outcomes, not missing; questionnaire missingness handled with multiple imputation as a sensitivity analysis.
  • Researcher degrees of freedom: pre-registration, frozen analysis script, open data/code, no optional stopping.
  • Effect sizes: odds ratios, geometric mean ratios, standardized differences, and precision; practical significance tied to the policy rule.

16.5 Falsification: what would prove the core idea wrong?

ObservationConclusion
No menu × state interaction, and one menu is best in every state.Emotion-contingent structural adaptation is unnecessary in this domain. The paper reports a robust global menu and discusses boundary conditions.
Interaction exists, but matched adaptation is not better than the static best menu.The cell-level differences are too small or the switching costs are too high. Adaptation is notworth the disruption in practice.
Matched and mismatched both beat static by the same amount.The benefit is change/novelty/attention, not matching. The theoretical claim of affective fit is not supported.

The strongest version of the paper

The strongest version does not need every hypothesis to be confirmed. It needs a clean 6 × 4 matrix, a credible induction, a causal test with the right counterfactuals, and an honest estimator evaluation. A paper that says “matching helps for frustration but not for confusion, and the estimator is not yet accurate enough to deploy” is more useful than one that claims a universal win.

16.6 Limitations to state explicitly

  1. The induced emotions are mild and temporary; they are not clinical states or necessarily the emotions of real shopping.
  2. “Menu structure” is a bundle; the study cannot isolate every visual or motor dimension.
  3. The mega menu has a click advantage; the analysis treats it as part of the structure and adds a matched-depth sensitivity analysis.
  4. The lab setting and fictional store limit ecological validity; search is disabled in the main task.
  5. The sample is not representative; cultural and age differences are not tested.
  6. The estimator is trained on a small, lab-generated dataset; its accuracy is an upper bound on what a real deployment could achieve without more data.
  7. The study measures short-term adaptation; long-term learning, trust, and habituation are future work.

19 Conclusion

The project’s contribution is not “emotion-aware menus” as a slogan. It is a disciplined answer to three questions: which menu structure fits which emotional context, whether using that mapping causally helps, and how accurate the emotion estimate must be before adaptation is worth it.

The plan is deliberately conservative. It treats emotion as a self-reported context, not a hidden truth. It treats menu structure as a bundle of geometry, breadth, depth, and persistence, and it measures the confounds rather than pretending they do not exist. It uses a static best menu, a mismatched menu, and a sham switch as counterfactuals, because “adaptive beats static” is not enough. It tests the estimator separately from the policy, because a good mapping with a bad estimator can still fail. And it pre-registers what would count as failure.

If the result is positive, the paper offers a validated mapping, a causal estimate, and a deployable policy with a confidence threshold. If the result is null, the paper still offers something useful: evidence that a stable menu is robust to mild emotional context, or that switching costs dominate, or that the estimator is the bottleneck. Either way, the study replaces a plausible intuition with evidence.

Appendix A — Task bank, difficulty rubric, and 24-task sample

A.1 Generation rules

  1. Every leaf product receives a unique attribute vector: case size, strap, movement, water resistance, dial colour, and price band.
  2. A task description uses two or three attributes and never the exact leaf label. It must map to exactly one product; the build-time uniqueness test rejects ambiguous descriptions.
  3. Tasks are written in natural shopping language (“You’re looking for…”), not menu language (“Go to Sport & Dive”).
  4. Each task is assigned a difficulty level by the rubric below; the final bank is balanced 24 easy / 24 medium / 24 hard.
  5. Each participant sees 24 tasks, balanced 8 / 8 / 8 across difficulty and across departments; the target set is rotated across participants.

Appendix B — Questionnaire items

B.1 Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM)

Valence: 9-point pictorial scale, 1 = very negative, 9 = very positive. Arousal: 9-point pictorial scale, 1 = very calm, 9 = very aroused. Anchor: “How do you feel right now?”

B.2 Discrete emotion check

“Right now, how much do you feel each of the following?” 7-point scale (1 = not at all, 7 = extremely): happy, frustrated, confused, bored. Add: “None of these describes how I feel” (checkbox). Dominant-state rule: the highest item must be ≥ 4 and at least 1 point above the second-highest; otherwise the state is labelled mixed.

B.3 NASA-TLX workload (frustration subscale excluded)

Four items, 0–100: mental demand (“How mentally demanding was the task?”), temporal demand (“How hurried or rushed was the pace?”), effort (“How hard did you have to work to accomplish your level of performance?”), performance (“How successful were you in accomplishing what you were asked to do?”; reverse-scored). Report the mean or weighted score; report the standard frustration item separately, never inside the workload composite.

B.4 UMUX-Lite

1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree. (1) “The interface’s capabilities meet my requirements.” (2) “The interface is easy to use.” Score: ((item1 − 1) + (item2 − 1)) / 12 × 100.

B.5 System Usability Scale (SUS)

10 items, 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree. Standard alternating positive/negative wording (Brooke, 1996). Score: odd items − 1; 5 − even items; sum × 2.5. Administer once per menu at the end of the session, with menu order counterbalanced.

B.6 Disorientation / control

7-point agreement: (1) “I felt lost.” (2) “I knew where I was in the store.” (3) “I could predict where to go next.” (4) “I felt in control of the navigation.” Items 2–4 reverse-scored for a disorientation score; also report the four items separately.

B.7 Engagement (short form)

7-point agreement: (1) “I was absorbed in the task.” (2) “The interface was aesthetically appealing.” (3) “The task felt worthwhile.” (4) “I wanted to continue exploring.” Adapted from O’Brien et al. (2018); report as a short engagement index, not as the full UES.

B.8 Trust / perceived intelligence (Study 2/3)

7-point agreement: (1) “The interface understood my needs.” (2) “The change was appropriate for the task.” (3) “I trusted the system.” (4) “The interface felt intelligent.”

B.9 Adaptation experience (Study 2/3)

(1) “I noticed that the interface changed.” (2) “I understood why it changed.” (3) “The change disrupted my flow.” (4) “I would want this adaptation in a real store.” (5) “I would prefer the previous layout.” Plus behavioural counts: keep-current clicks, switch-back clicks, dislike clicks.

B.10 Demographics and individual differences

Age; gender (open field); education; online shopping frequency (never / monthly / weekly / daily); watch expertise (1–7); tech savvy (1–7); 4-item need for cognition; 4-item ambiguity tolerance; colour-vision screening; prior experience with each menu type (1–7).

B.11 Suspicion check

Open-ended: “What did you think this study was about?” “Did you notice any pattern in the menus or the way they changed?” “Did you change your behaviour because you thought the study wanted you to?” Code as no hypothesis, partial, correct guess.

Appendix C — Induction scripts

Happy (≈ 2.5 min). “For the next two minutes, please watch this short clip. It’s a light comedy scene; there’s nothing you need to do except watch. You can look away or stop if you prefer.” [Play the clip.] “Thanks. Take a moment. How do you feel right now?” [SAM + discrete check.]
Frustration (≈ 2.5 min). “Please use the search box to find the ‘Titanium Deep’ watch. The search is still being tested, so it may be slow.” [The search fails with a generic “No results — try again” message.] “Please try once more.” [Second failure.] “One more try, please.” [Third failure.] “Thank you — that’s enough for this part. The search was designed to fail; it wasn’t your fault.” [Record frustration; offer a 30-s neutral pause before the trials.]
Confusion (≈ 2.5 min). “Here are six watches. Please sort them into the two categories you think the store uses. There’s no right answer — we’re interested in how people make sense of ambiguous categories. Talk me through your thinking as you go.” [The items are deliberately ambiguous: two could fit either category; no feedback is given.] “Thanks. That was intentionally ambiguous; the ambiguity is the point of this part.”
Boredom (≈ 90 s). “Please check the price of each watch on this list and write it on the sheet. It’s a routine data-entry task; accuracy matters more than speed.” [20 near-identical rows; no feedback; repeat until 90 s.] “Thanks — that’s the end of the data-entry task.”
Neutral reset (≈ 60 s). “Let’s take a short pause. Please watch the screen; there’s nothing to remember or do.” [Play 60 s of calm nature footage, no abrupt cuts or loud audio.] “When you’re ready, we’ll continue.”

Stop rules

Stop the induction immediately if the participant says “stop,” rates distress > 6/10, shows signs of panic or tearfulness, or asks to leave. Stay with them, offer water and a neutral activity, and debrief. Record the event and report it to the ethics committee. Never continue a block “to complete the data.”

Appendix I — Glossary

TermDefinition in this plan
Adaptive navigationA system that changes the menu structure, content, or presentation in response to the user’s state or behaviour.
Affective fitThe hypothesis that a menu’s structural demands should match the processing style associated with the current emotional context.
BreadthThe number of options visible at a given menu level.
DepthThe number of levels or clicks required to reach a leaf.
Fractal menuA recursive layout in which the same spatial pattern repeats at each level; selecting a parent zooms into its children.
Mega menuA wide panel that shows multiple subcategories and leaves at once, usually two clicks from the top level.
Radial latticeA menu arranged around a centre, with options at compass sectors and concentric rings.
RS gridA grid of large rounded-square icon cards, colour-coded by category.
State labelThe dominant self-reported emotion after induction, or mixed if no item dominates.
Matched conditionThe menu selected by the Study 1 policy for the current state.
Mismatched conditionA pre-registered derangement of the state→menu mapping; controls for change without fit.
Sham conditionA banner/announcement of change with no actual layout change; controls for demand and attention.
Break-even accuracyThe estimator accuracy at which the expected utility of hybrid adaptation equals that of the static best menu.
