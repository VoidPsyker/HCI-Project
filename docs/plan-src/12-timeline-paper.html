18 Paper outline and publication plan

The paper should be written as an empirical HCI paper with a clear causal claim and a honest estimator evaluation — not as a design showcase. The title, abstract, and contribution statement should foreground the 6 × 4 mapping and the matched-vs-static test.

18.1 Title and abstract

Working title: “Emotion-Contingent Navigation: A Controlled Comparison of Six Menu Structures and a Causal Test of Adaptive Switching.”

Alternative: “Does the Menu Need to Change? Mapping and Testing Emotion-Contingent Navigation in E-Commerce.”

Abstract structure (200–250 words): (1) problem — static menus, emotional context, adaptive interfaces; (2) gap — no controlled test of whole-structure adaptation; (3) method — pilot, 6 × 4 within-subject mapping, causal test with static/matched/mismatched/sham, end-to-end estimator; (4) results — the key pattern (to be filled); (5) implication — when adaptation helps, when it does not, and the accuracy threshold.

18.3 Contribution statement (draft)

  1. We provide the first controlled 6 × 4 mapping of menu-structure performance and experience across experimentally induced emotional contexts in e-commerce.
  2. We separate matching from mere change using static, matched, mismatched, and sham conditions, and we quantify switching costs.
  3. We show how to derive a policy from the mapping with explicit uncertainty, and we test it end-to-end with an imperfect estimator.
  4. We report the estimator accuracy at which adaptation stops paying for itself, giving designers a concrete threshold rather than a slogan.
  5. We release a reproducible six-menu prototype, task bank, event schema, and analysis pipeline.

18.5 Reviewer objections and planned responses

Likely objectionResponse / design feature
“The menus differ in click depth; the comparison is unfair.”Click depth is a real property of mega menus. Report it, mediate it, and add a matched-depth sensitivity analysis on A/B/D/E/F.
“Induced emotions are not real shopping emotions.”Acknowledge; use manipulation checks, dose-response, and a clear scope statement. A field study is future work.
“Six menus × four states is underpowered.”Report the simulation, the achieved precision, and the focused contrasts; do not overclaim small effects. Use N=72 where possible.
“The adaptive gain could be novelty.”The mismatched and sham conditions directly test this; report the pattern honestly.
“The estimator is trained and tested on lab data.”Participant-grouped CV, frozen pipeline, calibration, break-even curve; discuss deployment gap.
“Why these four emotions?”Valence × arousal + cognitive/motivational distinctness; alternatives considered; manipulation checks and discriminant validity.
“Why these six menus?”Coverage of linear, panel, recursive, icon-grid, and radial geometries; each has a theoretical rationale; the set is not exhaustive.
“Where is the theory?”Cognitive load, wayfinding/spatial memory, visual salience, motor efficiency; mediation tests; predictions in Table 1.2.

18.6 Publication strategy

Paper 1 (core): Study 1 + Study 2, with the estimator break-even simulation as a bridge. This is a complete CHI/IUI paper: mapping, causal test, and a practical threshold. Paper 2 (extension): Study 3 end-to-end with the full estimator, calibration, and field deployment. If time is short, publish Paper 1 and treat Study 3 as a thesis chapter or a follow-up. Do not wait for the perfect three-study paper if the first two are solid; the marginal gain from a small end-to-end study may not justify the delay.

18.7 Open science

  • Pre-register on OSF before data collection; update with deviations and their reasons.
  • Release the prototype, taxonomy, task bank, event schema, and analysis code under a permissive licence.
  • Share anonymised trial-level data and questionnaires if ethics allow; otherwise share a synthetic dataset and the code.
  • Report all conditions, all pre-registered outcomes, and all exclusions; do not hide null results.
  • Archive a DOI (Zenodo) for the exact manuscript version.

19 Conclusion

The project’s contribution is not “emotion-aware menus” as a slogan. It is a disciplined answer to three questions: which menu structure fits which emotional context, whether using that mapping causally helps, and how accurate the emotion estimate must be before adaptation is worth it.

The plan is deliberately conservative. It treats emotion as a self-reported context, not a hidden truth. It treats menu structure as a bundle of geometry, breadth, depth, and persistence, and it measures the confounds rather than pretending they do not exist. It uses a static best menu, a mismatched menu, and a sham switch as counterfactuals, because “adaptive beats static” is not enough. It tests the estimator separately from the policy, because a good mapping with a bad estimator can still fail. And it pre-registers what would count as failure.

If the result is positive, the paper offers a validated mapping, a causal estimate, and a deployable policy with a confidence threshold. If the result is null, the paper still offers something useful: evidence that a stable menu is robust to mild emotional context, or that switching costs dominate, or that the estimator is the bottleneck. Either way, the study replaces a plausible intuition with evidence.
