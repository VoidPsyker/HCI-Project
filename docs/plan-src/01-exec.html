0 Executive summary

The project asks a simple but surprisingly untested question: if a user’s emotional context changes, should the navigation structure change with it? Most e-commerce sites keep one menu architecture for everyone. Most adaptive-menu research changes item order, highlights, or recommendations inside a fixed structure. This project compares six genuinely different structures — horizontal bar, vertical sidebar, mega menu, fractal zoom, rounded-square icon grid, and radial lattice — under four experimentally induced emotional contexts, then tests whether switching structure on the basis of that mapping actually improves product finding.

0.1 The scientific gap

Four literatures meet here but do not yet connect. Menu research shows that breadth, depth, layout, and visual format change search speed and errors (Miller, 1981; Kiger, 1984; Leuthold et al., 2011; Chiu & Yang, 2024). Adaptive-interface research shows that personalisation can improve satisfaction and engagement, but also that reordering and switching create predictability and learning costs (Findlater & McGrenere, 2004; Gajos et al., 2008; Lavie & Meyer, 2010; Gaspar-Figueiredo et al., 2025). Emotion research shows that affect shapes information processing and search persistence (Russell, 1980; Schwarz & Clore, 1983; Isen, 2001). Navigation research shows that disorientation and frustration are central failure modes (Lazar et al., 2003; Smith, 1996). What is missing is a controlled test that treats whole menu structure as the adaptive variable and emotional context as the moderator.

The existing protocol (v3.0) proposed four menus and four emotions. This plan extends it in five ways: (1) adds the rounded-square icon grid and the radial lattice, giving six structures that cover linear, panel, recursive-spatial, icon-grid, and radial geometries; (2) formalises the 6 × 4 design, randomisation, and target rotation; (3) replaces optimistic sample sizes with a simulation-based power analysis; (4) adds a causal test that separates matching from mere switching; and (5) adds an explicit estimator-error simulation so the end-to-end study can answer “how accurate must emotion inference be before adaptation pays off?”

0.2 The four emotional contexts

We keep happiness / positive affect and frustration, and add two states that are specifically diagnostic for navigation: confusion / lostness and boredom / disengagement. Together they span the two dimensions of the affect circumplex (valence and arousal) plus two states that matter uniquely for wayfinding: uncertainty about where something is (confusion) and low motivation to keep looking (boredom). Anxiety and curiosity were considered but rejected for the core design; §3.2 explains why.

0.3 The six menus

All six render the same four departments, three subcategories per department, six leaf styles per subcategory, and the same 72 fictional watches. The running example is Sport & Dive → Pro Diver 300m → Titanium Deep.

What makes this a paper rather than a prototype demo

The paper does not assume “frustrated users need a sidebar.” Study 1 estimates the 6 × 4 mapping. Study 2 then freezes that mapping and tests whether using it beats (a) the best static menu and (b) a deliberately mismatched menu. Study 3 tests the complete system with a noisy estimator and a safe transition policy. A null result — for example, one menu wins everywhere, or switching costs cancel the gains — is a publishable finding because it answers the applied question “is emotion-contingent structural adaptation worth it?”

0.5 Primary outcomes

Performance: task success (binary), completion time (log-transformed; censored at timeout), navigation errors, backtracking, category switches, click count, and path efficiency. Experience: perceived usability, cognitive load, perceived control, predictability, disorientation, satisfaction, and preference. Adaptation: switch acceptance, reversion, perceived disruption, trust, and the cost of the first trial after a switch. Estimation: macro-F1, balanced accuracy, per-class recall, confusion matrix, calibration, and decision-curve net benefit.

0.6 Feasibility and decisions

Full programme

N ≈ 234 across three studies; 6–9 months part-time; lab with a 1920 × 1080 desktop; optional eye tracker; ethics approval; one research assistant for counterbalancing and data checks.

Reduced programme (recommended for a semester)

Pilot + Study 1 (N = 48) + Study 2 (N = 60). Drop Study 3 or run it as a small feasibility study (N = 24) without a deployable estimator. This still yields a complete paper: mapping + causal test + a simulation of estimator error.

Minimum publishable unit

Pilot + Study 1 with N = 48 and a focused test of four pre-registered state-specific contrasts. This is enough for a CHI Extended Abstract or a thesis chapter, but the causal claim is weaker.

What the plan deliberately does not do

  • It does not use webcam, facial expression, or biometric “emotion detection.” Self-report anchors the state; interaction signals are treated as noisy evidence.
  • It does not assume that one menu is “best” before the data. Directional predictions are stated, but the mapping is estimated.
  • It does not change the menu mid-route. Switches happen at task boundaries, with an explanation and a keep-current option in Study 3.
  • It does not hide null results. A global winner, a failed induction, or a switching-cost null are all informative.

Three things to confirm before building

(1) Timeline and sample access. The full design needs ~234 participants; the reduced design ~118. If you cannot reach that, use Track A in §17 and pre-register a focused, underpowered-by-design study with precision reporting. (2) Equipment. Eye tracking is optional; the core plan uses interaction logs and self-report. (3) Citation status. The Chiossi et al. (CHI 2026) and Collins et al. (CHI 2026) references could not be verified. Menus E and F are defined operationally here; do not cite unverified claims in the paper.
