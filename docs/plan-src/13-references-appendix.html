Appendix A — Task bank, difficulty rubric, and 24-task sample

A.1 Generation rules

  1. Every leaf product receives a unique attribute vector: case size, strap, movement, water resistance, dial colour, and price band.
  2. A task description uses two or three attributes and never the exact leaf label. It must map to exactly one product; the build-time uniqueness test rejects ambiguous descriptions.
  3. Tasks are written in natural shopping language (“You’re looking for…”), not menu language (“Go to Sport & Dive”).
  4. Each task is assigned a difficulty level by the rubric below; the final bank is balanced 24 easy / 24 medium / 24 hard.
  5. Each participant sees 24 tasks, balanced 8 / 8 / 8 across difficulty and across departments; the target set is rotated across participants.

A.2 Difficulty rubric

EasyDepartment 1–2; subcategory 1; leaf 1–2.Task words overlap strongly with the leaf label.0–1 same-colour or same-material leaves.
MediumDepartment 2–3; subcategory 2; leaf 3–4.One attribute must be translated (e.g., “lightweight” → titanium).2–3 plausible leaves in the same subcategory.
HardDepartment 3–4; subcategory 3; leaf 5–6.Two attributes are indirect; the label uses a metaphor or material term.4+ plausible leaves across subcategories.

Appendix B — Questionnaire items

B.1 Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM)

Valence: 9-point pictorial scale, 1 = very negative, 9 = very positive. Arousal: 9-point pictorial scale, 1 = very calm, 9 = very aroused. Anchor: “How do you feel right now?”

B.2 Discrete emotion check

“Right now, how much do you feel each of the following?” 7-point scale (1 = not at all, 7 = extremely): happy, frustrated, confused, bored. Add: “None of these describes how I feel” (checkbox). Dominant-state rule: the highest item must be ≥ 4 and at least 1 point above the second-highest; otherwise the state is labelled mixed.

B.3 NASA-TLX workload (frustration subscale excluded)

Four items, 0–100: mental demand (“How mentally demanding was the task?”), temporal demand (“How hurried or rushed was the pace?”), effort (“How hard did you have to work to accomplish your level of performance?”), performance (“How successful were you in accomplishing what you were asked to do?”; reverse-scored). Report the mean or weighted score; report the standard frustration item separately, never inside the workload composite.

B.4 UMUX-Lite

1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree. (1) “The interface’s capabilities meet my requirements.” (2) “The interface is easy to use.” Score: ((item1 − 1) + (item2 − 1)) / 12 × 100.

B.5 System Usability Scale (SUS)

10 items, 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree. Standard alternating positive/negative wording (Brooke, 1996). Score: odd items − 1; 5 − even items; sum × 2.5. Administer once per menu at the end of the session, with menu order counterbalanced.

B.6 Disorientation / control

7-point agreement: (1) “I felt lost.” (2) “I knew where I was in the store.” (3) “I could predict where to go next.” (4) “I felt in control of the navigation.” Items 2–4 reverse-scored for a disorientation score; also report the four items separately.

B.7 Engagement (short form)

7-point agreement: (1) “I was absorbed in the task.” (2) “The interface was aesthetically appealing.” (3) “The task felt worthwhile.” (4) “I wanted to continue exploring.” Adapted from O’Brien et al. (2018); report as a short engagement index, not as the full UES.

B.8 Trust / perceived intelligence (Study 2/3)

7-point agreement: (1) “The interface understood my needs.” (2) “The change was appropriate for the task.” (3) “I trusted the system.” (4) “The interface felt intelligent.”

B.9 Adaptation experience (Study 2/3)

(1) “I noticed that the interface changed.” (2) “I understood why it changed.” (3) “The change disrupted my flow.” (4) “I would want this adaptation in a real store.” (5) “I would prefer the previous layout.” Plus behavioural counts: keep-current clicks, switch-back clicks, dislike clicks.

B.10 Demographics and individual differences

Age; gender (open field); education; online shopping frequency (never / monthly / weekly / daily); watch expertise (1–7); tech savvy (1–7); 4-item need for cognition; 4-item ambiguity tolerance; colour-vision screening; prior experience with each menu type (1–7).

B.11 Suspicion check

Open-ended: “What did you think this study was about?” “Did you notice any pattern in the menus or the way they changed?” “Did you change your behaviour because you thought the study wanted you to?” Code as no hypothesis, partial, correct guess.

Appendix C — Induction scripts

Happy (≈ 2.5 min). “For the next two minutes, please watch this short clip. It’s a light comedy scene; there’s nothing you need to do except watch. You can look away or stop if you prefer.” [Play the clip.] “Thanks. Take a moment. How do you feel right now?” [SAM + discrete check.]
Frustration (≈ 2.5 min). “Please use the search box to find the ‘Titanium Deep’ watch. The search is still being tested, so it may be slow.” [The search fails with a generic “No results — try again” message.] “Please try once more.” [Second failure.] “One more try, please.” [Third failure.] “Thank you — that’s enough for this part. The search was designed to fail; it wasn’t your fault.” [Record frustration; offer a 30-s neutral pause before the trials.]
Confusion (≈ 2.5 min). “Here are six watches. Please sort them into the two categories you think the store uses. There’s no right answer — we’re interested in how people make sense of ambiguous categories. Talk me through your thinking as you go.” [The items are deliberately ambiguous: two could fit either category; no feedback is given.] “Thanks. That was intentionally ambiguous; the ambiguity is the point of this part.”
Boredom (≈ 90 s). “Please check the price of each watch on this list and write it on the sheet. It’s a routine data-entry task; accuracy matters more than speed.” [20 near-identical rows; no feedback; repeat until 90 s.] “Thanks — that’s the end of the data-entry task.”
Neutral reset (≈ 60 s). “Let’s take a short pause. Please watch the screen; there’s nothing to remember or do.” [Play 60 s of calm nature footage, no abrupt cuts or loud audio.] “When you’re ready, we’ll continue.”

Stop rules

Stop the induction immediately if the participant says “stop,” rates distress > 6/10, shows signs of panic or tearfulness, or asks to leave. Stay with them, offer water and a neutral activity, and debrief. Record the event and report it to the ethics committee. Never continue a block “to complete the data.”

Appendix G — Pilot QA checklist

  • All 72 products reachable in all six menus; no dead ends; no overlapping hit targets below 44 × 44 px.
  • Every task description maps to exactly one product; the uniqueness test passes for the full bank.
  • Event log reconstructs every practice and main trial; no missing trial_end; no duplicate event_id.
  • Randomisation balance: each block order, menu position, and target set used approximately equally.
  • Questionnaire flow: no missing items; skip logic correct; timing acceptable; mobile/desktop rendering checked.
  • Induction: dominant-state rate ≥ 60%; mean intensity ≥ 5/7; no distress > 6/10; stop rules understood by the experimenter.
  • Task difficulty: overall success 75–90%; easy > medium > hard; no floor or ceiling.
  • Timing: session ≤ 100 min; trial median ≤ 45 s; frame rate ≥ 50 fps; no long tasks during a trial.
  • Accessibility: keyboard path works; focus visible; contrast passes; reduced motion respected; axe-core clean.
  • Data: offline queue survives a network drop; backup restores; export checksum matches.
  • Debrief: deception explained; mood checked; support resources provided; withdrawal option clear.
  • Freeze: build hash, taxonomy hash, questionnaire version, and analysis script version recorded.

Appendix H — Consent and debrief outline

H.1 Information sheet (plain language)

  • Purpose: compare website navigation layouts for finding products.
  • What you will do: product-finding tasks, short mood exercises (a comedy clip, a search task that may not work, an ambiguous sorting task, a repetitive catalogue task), questionnaires.
  • Time: about 90 minutes (Study 1), 65 minutes (Study 2), 70 minutes (Study 3).
  • Data: clicks, timing, questionnaire responses; optional eye tracking in a separate session.
  • Risks: mild, temporary frustration, confusion, or boredom; you can stop at any time.
  • Compensation: £12–£15/hour or course credit with an alternative; paid even if you withdraw.
  • Privacy: pseudonymous ID; no names or emails in the research data; retention per ethics approval.
  • Contact: PI name, email, ethics committee contact, support resources.

H.2 Debrief script

“Thank you for taking part. I can now explain the full purpose. We are comparing six different menu layouts and asking whether the best layout depends on how someone feels. The search task earlier was designed to fail — that was part of the study, not your mistake. The sorting task was deliberately ambiguous, and the catalogue task was deliberately repetitive. We used these short exercises to create mild, temporary states; they are not a test of you. The interface sometimes changed between tasks; we were testing whether that change helped or disrupted you. You can withdraw your data now without giving a reason. Do you have any questions? How are you feeling now?” [Offer a neutral-positive closing task and support resources.]

Appendix I — Glossary

TermDefinition in this plan
Adaptive navigationA system that changes the menu structure, content, or presentation in response to the user’s state or behaviour.
Affective fitThe hypothesis that a menu’s structural demands should match the processing style associated with the current emotional context.
BreadthThe number of options visible at a given menu level.
DepthThe number of levels or clicks required to reach a leaf.
Fractal menuA recursive layout in which the same spatial pattern repeats at each level; selecting a parent zooms into its children.
Mega menuA wide panel that shows multiple subcategories and leaves at once, usually two clicks from the top level.
Radial latticeA menu arranged around a centre, with options at compass sectors and concentric rings.
RS gridA grid of large rounded-square icon cards, colour-coded by category.
State labelThe dominant self-reported emotion after induction, or mixed if no item dominates.
Matched conditionThe menu selected by the Study 1 policy for the current state.
Mismatched conditionA pre-registered derangement of the state→menu mapping; controls for change without fit.
Sham conditionA banner/announcement of change with no actual layout change; controls for demand and attention.
Break-even accuracyThe estimator accuracy at which the expected utility of hybrid adaptation equals that of the static best menu.
